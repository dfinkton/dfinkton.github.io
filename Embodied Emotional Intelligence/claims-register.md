# Claims Register — Lesson 2, Emotional Awareness

One row per claim, not per source. The source ledger records what each paper says; this
file records **what we currently believe and how much weight it can hold.** Update the
status when evidence lands; delete a claim outright when it is debunked rather than leaving
it struck through.

**Status vocabulary**
- `VALIDATED` — multiple independent sources, no serious contradiction found
- `SUPPORTED` — real evidence, not yet replicated independently or with a known weakness
- `CONTESTED` — good evidence pointing both ways
- `OPEN` — plausible, insufficient evidence either way
- `RETIRED` — checked and found false or unsupportable; kept so it is not re-adopted
- `UNSEARCHED` — an **absence claim** ("no study has done X") reached by not finding something
  in what we happened to read, rather than by a deliberate search. **Not usable until searched.**

## Process rule: absence of evidence requires a search, not a gap in our reading

Twice on 13 Aug I asserted that something had never been studied — partner-matching as a
moderator of emotional awareness, and training arousal-state identification. The first was
**already in our own source ledger**, misfiled as an anomaly under O4 instead of as a finding.
The second was found by a single web search, which also turned up **objective**
interoceptive-accuracy gains (5-day biofeedback training, heartbeat detection) that I had
recorded as unestablished.

**Consequence:** no claim of the form "X has no evidence" enters this register without a
documented search behind it. Existing absence claims are tagged `UNSEARCHED` until one is run.
A gap in our reading is not a gap in the literature.

**The working premise, as restated by Darryl 13 Aug 2026 — this replaces "people tend to know
how they feel":**

> **People can feel their feelings. They may not have the words, but they feel them, and those
> feelings drive their actions.**

This is a materially different and better-supported claim than the one it replaces, and it
dissolves the need for a population-baseline study on accurate identification:
- It does not assert accurate *identification*, so Lundh (~20% reporting low alexithymia
  performed worse on the LEAS; 15% reporting high performed better) and Leising 2009 (people
  reporting more difficulty describing emotions were *better* at expressing them) no longer
  contradict it — those results are about self-assessed awareness, not about feeling.
- **"Feelings drive actions" is directly supported, and better supported than "awareness drives
  actions":** Kalokerinos — people *lower* in granularity used reappraisal and social sharing
  **more**, not less, and the strategies worked less well. They acted; labeling wasn't the gate.
  Hamilton 2025 — **affect variability predicted while granularity did not**. Kircanski — skin
  conductance moved d=0.64–0.85 while self-reported fear did not; the body carried the effect.
  Compas — the three significant strategies (avoidance .17, suppression .13, denial .10) are all
  *things people do in response to feeling*, and they predict symptoms.
- It relocates the lesson's work from "learn to identify what you feel" to **"you already feel
  it and it is already moving you — the question is what happens next,"** which is where our
  strongest evidence actually sits (Compas's factor level, secondary control coping −.25/−.30).

**The prior scope question, unchanged:** are there outcomes associated with emotional awareness
— **with or without verbally naming it** — are there practices that shift those associations,
and does awareness of a specific emotion help a person manage that emotion, including inside
other interventions.

## Process rule: web-fetch summaries are not evidence

A fetch-and-summarize pass on Tugade & Fredrickson 2004 reported that positive granularity
"predicted resilience independent of negative granularity and trait resilience" and
"better coping and lower depressive symptoms during stress." **None of that is in the
paper.** It was invented. Reading the PDF showed four significant correlations under r=.27,
all against a self-report coping questionnaire, with no health or symptom outcome measured
anywhere.

**Consequence:** a claim only enters this register at `SUPPORTED` or higher once the
primary text has been read directly. Fetch summaries are leads — they say a paper exists
and roughly what it is about. Any entry sourced from one is capped at `OPEN` and marked.
Several current entries are in that position and are flagged.

## D. What no row above shows — the anti-overreach line

**Every positive result in this map is measured on one of three things:** a questionnaire about the
skill (TAS-20, MAIA, LEAS), a lab or EMA measure of the skill itself (differentiation ICC, heartbeat
detection, amygdala response), or **one** physiological outcome in **one** paradigm (skin conductance,
Kircanski).

**Nothing here measures symptoms, wellbeing, relationships, or behaviour in daily life.** Where that
*has* been measured at scale, the result is not encouraging: **Compas 2017, 212 studies, N=80,850 —
emotional expression × internalising symptoms r = .08, k=6, ns.**

**Be precise about Compas's longitudinal result — I had been vague about it.** Its longitudinal subset
is **17 observational studies** where a **coping style was measured at Time 1 and symptoms at Time 2.
There is no practice in Compas, so it cannot speak to whether the benefits of a practice last.** The
authors' own reading: protective factors "**may only be correlates of symptoms when measured at the
same point in time.**" **The claim it supports is that a coping style measured today does not predict
symptoms later — not that trained gains decay.** See the durability note in `source-ledger.md`.

**And on durability proper, split three ways:** *with* continued practice, objective interoceptive
accuracy kept climbing over 9 months (d=.173 → .273) while thought content and affect did **not**
accumulate over 10 weeks and TAS-20 **did** drop over the same 10 weeks. *Without* continued practice,
the only data are three single-session follow-ups — 1 week (Kircanski), 48 hours (Nils & Rimé), 1–2
days (naming-before-reappraising). **For every multi-week practice here, what happens after stopping
is unmeasured. "We don't know" is the accurate statement, not "it fades."**

**So the defensible claim shape is:** *this practice moves this measure of this skill, in this
population, by roughly this much.* **Not** *this practice makes people better off.* The second claim
has no support in anything read for this lesson.

## E. Two population facts that bound everything above
1. **Bornemann, Silveira, Petzold and Bornemann & Singer all screened out clinical cases** —
   including TAS-20 > 60. So the body-practice evidence comes from the population where **Trevisan
   2019 (66 samples, N=7,146) found the body–emotion correlation to be non-significant.**
2. **Mean ages 40–48 throughout, 70–78% female, German or US, highly educated.** The one clinical
   sample (Widdershoven) is medicated MDD outpatients. **There is no adolescent or young-adult
   evidence for any practice in this map.**

## 1. Tier A — heavy evidence, and it constrains what the lesson may promise

**A1. "Emotional awareness" is at least four constructs that do not converge. `VALIDATED`**
TAS-20 × LEAS **r = −0.122**, N=2,857, 28 samples (Maroti, full text read). Plus Garfinkel, Dunning,
Thompson. **Leverage: total.** Governs every sentence. Not taught — it disciplines the prose.

**A2. Self-report moves; performance and behaviour move less. `VALIDATED`**
Multiple independent literatures. **This is the single most useful predictor of whether a finding
will hold**, and it explains most apparent contradictions below.

**A3. Self-reported body awareness and self-reported emotional awareness correlate weakly, and
not at all in healthy samples. `VALIDATED — abstract read verbatim 14 Aug`**
**Trevisan, Altschuler, Bagdasarov, Carlos, Duan, Hamo, Kala, McNair, Parker, Stahl, Winkelman, Zhou
& McPartland (2019)**, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology* 128(8):765–776, PMID 31380655.
**66 independent samples, N = 7,146. r = −.162, p=.001, 95% CI [−.252, −.068].** Verbatim:
"the relationship was **nonsignificant in healthy, typically developing samples**."

**What it bounds:** a claim that scoring higher on a body-awareness questionnaire goes with
scoring higher on an emotional-awareness questionnaire. It is a correlation between two
self-report instruments in cross-sectional samples.

**What it does not bound:** whether attending to the body is a useful route into noticing an
emotion. That is a practice question and A4 is the direct test of it — the MAIA subscale defined
as the sensation-to-emotion link did move with body scan.

**A4. A body scan does not improve raw detection of sensation — but it does improve the felt
connection between sensation and emotion. `VALIDATED — full text read 14 Aug`**
**Bornemann, Herbert, Mehling & Singer (2014)**, *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:1504, PMID 25610410,
DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01504. **Full text read.** n=148 in the ReSource Presence module, retest
control; MAIA validated on n=1,076.

**CORRECTION, 14 Aug.** This register said "regulatory facets moved, noticing did not," which was
true but hid the finding that matters most to this lesson. **Naming the eight subscales changes the
conclusion.**

| MAIA subscale | Authors' definition | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-Regulation** | "ability to **regulate distress by attention to body sensations**" | **moved, d = 0.72** |
| **Attention Regulation** | "ability to sustain and control attention to body sensations" | **moved, d = 0.54** |
| **Body Listening** | "active listening to the body for insight" | **moved, d = 0.40** |
| **Trusting** | "experience of one's body as safe and trustworthy" | **moved, F=14.90, p<.001, d ≈ 0.29** |
| **Emotional Awareness** | **"awareness of the connection between body sensations and emotional states"** — item: *"I notice how my body changes when I am angry"* | **moved, but weakest and barely: F(226)=4.34, p=.04, d ≈ 0.17** |
| **Noticing** | "awareness of uncomfortable, comfortable, and neutral body sensations" | **did not move — d = 0.19, interaction ns** |
| Not-Worrying | "tendency not to worry… with sensations of pain or discomfort" | did not move |
| Not-Distracting | "tendency not to ignore or distract oneself from sensations of pain" | did not move |

Significant interactions at F ≥ 4.34, p ≤ 0.04; non-significant ones at p ≥ 0.11.

**What this actually says: three months of body scan did not make people better at detecting
sensations. It made them better at connecting sensations to emotions, at holding attention on the
body, at using the body to settle, and at trusting it.** The subscale literally named "Emotional
Awareness," and defined as the sensation→emotion link, **is one of the five that moved.** That is
Lesson 2's target, and I had been reporting it as though the target had not moved.

**The dose is larger than "brief" implies:** **5×/week, 30 minutes (20 min Body Scan + 10 min Breath
Meditation)** at home, **plus weekly 2-hour classes for 13 weeks**; mean total practice
**36.48 hours (SD 10.85).**

**The predictors are the most actionable finding in this audit, and they are not duration.**
- **Practice duration: weak.** Predicted only Self-Regulation (r=0.22, p=.007) and Trusting
  (r=0.20, p=.013).
- **Liking the practice: predicted change on six of eight scales**, rs 0.19–0.43, all p<.05.
- **Integrating it into daily life: predicted change on seven of eight scales**, similar range.
- Together they explained **26% of the variance in Self-Regulation change**, F(3,135)=15.80, p<.001.

**Consistent with [[A3]]:** the thing that did not move — Noticing — is "the aspect that is
predominantly assessed in other IA measures," which is part of why the population-level body/emotion
correlation is so small. **The instruments have mostly been measuring the facet that doesn't train.**
Corroborated in direction by Silveira 2023, where only the dyad raised body listening and
self-regulation. **Leverage: very high.** This is the direct test of the lesson's practice, and it
supports it more specifically than this register previously said.

**A5. Emotional expression is null against youth symptoms, and protective associations collapse
longitudinally. `VALIDATED — full text read`**
Compas et al. 2017, *Psychological Bulletin*, **212 studies, N=80,850.** Emotional expression ×
internalising **r = .08, k=6, ns**. Longitudinally: emotion regulation p=.52, primary control p=.58,
secondary control p=.90. **Leverage: high** — it is the reason this lesson cannot promise outcomes.

**A6. Effect sizes shrink clinical → general → student. `VALIDATED`** Sets transfer for everything
else. Note this compounds A3: the body/emotion link is a clinical-population phenomenon.

## 2. Tier B — solid, and directly usable in the lesson

**B1. The seven-category vocabulary maps onto the Big Five. `VALIDATED — citation now verified;
abstract not yet read verbatim`**
**CORRECTION AND UPGRADE.** Recorded as "a 2021 *Scientific Reports* meta-analysis" with no
citation. **Found: Marengo D, Davis KL, Özkarar Gradwohl G & Montag C (2021), "A meta-analysis on
individual differences in primary emotional systems and Big Five personality traits,"
*Scientific Reports* 11(1):7453, PMID 33811242, PMC8018956, DOI 10.1038/s41598-021-84366-8.
21 samples.** Mappings confirmed: high SEEKING→Openness, high PLAY→Extraversion,
high CARE/low ANGER→Agreeableness, high FEAR/SADNESS/ANGER→Neuroticism.

**But the important detail, which changes what can be claimed: the ANPS measures SIX systems —
SEEKING, PLAY, CARE, FEAR, SADNESS, ANGER. LUST IS NOT IN THE INSTRUMENT.** So the validated claim
is *six of the seven map onto the Big Five*; **LUST is unmeasured by the instrument that validates
the scheme.** Since LUST gets its own lesson (17), this matters there too.
**Still outstanding:** no effect sizes, and I have the mapping via search rather than a verbatim
abstract — which my own rule says is insufficient. **Get the abstract.**

**B2. Basic emotion words beat fine-grained ones; abstraction matters, intensity doesn't.
`SUPPORTED — primary text read, N=20`**
Torre 2016 dissertation. "Angry" produced more amygdala reduction than "irritated" or "enraged";
intensity did nothing. **Leverage: very high, evidence thin.** It is the only experimental
granularity result we have and it favours **fewer, coarser** words — i.e. it supports the seven
directly. **Do not print the fMRI numbers as though replicated.**

**B3. Provided words work differently from self-generated ones. `SUPPORTED — four literatures`**
Torre (fMRI, provided labels deactivate amygdala, p=.004); Sheeran (self-specified cues **d=.16** vs
provided .31–1.17); Breitwieser (plan quality); Bayer & Gollwitzer (information-only control).
**Design rule for the lesson: hand them the seven; don't ask them to generate vocabulary.**
**Caveat that must travel with it:** Barrett treats the provided-word-list advantage in
*other*-directed recognition as an **artifact that inflates apparent accuracy**. Provided words are
good pedagogy for your own experience; they may manufacture false confidence about other people.

**B4. Repeatedly rating specific emotions moves negative differentiation. `SUPPORTED — full text
read`**
Widdershoven 2019: **d = 0.43**, controlled, n=55 vs 24; 6 weeks × 3 days/week × 10 ratings/day of
12 named emotions. **The intervention *was* the practice** — no teaching. **No dose-response**
(p=.117, restricted range). **Positive differentiation did not move** — the fifth independent null on
that. **Leverage: very high.** This is the closest thing to evidence for the lesson's practice.

**B5. Where people feel emotions in the body is mappable and cross-culturally stable.
`VALIDATED — full text read`**
Nummenmaa, Glerean, Hari & Hietanen 2014, *PNAS*. **701 participants**, emBODY task; classifiers
above chance (72%/38% vs 50%/14%); maps concordant across Finnish, Swedish and Taiwanese samples,
**mean rs = 0.70.** Authors' caveat: may reflect **learned conceptual/linguistic association** rather
than physiological patterning, and cannot link maps to an underlying activation pattern.
**AUDIT GAP FOUND: this is not in the claims register at all** — it lives only in the ledger, with a
standing note that it is uncited in the lesson notes. **It is the empirical backbone of a body-first
practice and needs its own claim entry.**

**B6. More awareness is not monotonically better. `SUPPORTED — now four sources`**
- Aaron, Blain, Snodgress & Park 2020, *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 11:132, PMID 32210852 (**authors
  resolved this audit**): alexithymia × interoceptive accuracy **quadratic p=.002**; sensibility
  linear p=.040. High alexithymia went with **either high or low** accuracy.
- Trevisan, Mehling & McPartland 2021, *Autism Research* 14(2):240–247, PMID 33336935: separates
  "reduced **adaptive** interoceptive attention" from "heightened **maladaptive** interoceptive
  attention related to anxiety-induced somatization."
- Luminet & Nielson 2025, *Annual Review of Psychology* 76:741–769, PMID 39322432: calls for
  distinguishing "emotion deficits and **emotion over-responding, including when over-responding is
  functional**."
- **NEW, found this audit:** Goldman CM, Chuning AE, Lane RD, Smith R & Weihs KL (2025),
  *Journal of Health Psychology* 30(13):3803–3819, PMID 39449575. **N=460 women with breast cancer,
  one-year longitudinal.** Verbatim: "In those with **higher EA, low social support was associated
  with greater depression and lower optimism**," and "**higher EA predicted greater depression at
  baseline** but lower depression at nine-month follow-up." Their reading: EA **increases sensitivity
  to available social support.**

**Leverage: very high.** Four independent lines say the same thing: **awareness is conditionally
useful, and for one subgroup "notice more" is the wrong instruction.** This is now one of the
best-supported claims in the file and the lesson should carry it.

**B7. People cannot tell whether an emotional intervention worked. `VALIDATED — three independent
demonstrations`** Nils & Rimé (reported three benefits, got none); Torre (expected labeling to feel
worse, it reduced amygdala response); Schweiger Gallo (goal group believed it outperformed the group
that improved, 6.61 vs 5.22). **Leverage: high**, and it is honest content the lesson can teach.

## 3. Tier C — real but qualified, and the lesson should hedge

**C1. Emotional awareness can be trained. `UPGRADED this audit from WEAKENED to SUPPORTED`**
**"Killgore 2020" is resolved and it is not what was claimed.** The real source:
**Smith R, Persich MR, Chuning AE, Cloonan S, Woods-Lubert R, Skalamera J, Berryhill SM, Weihs KL,
Lane RD, Allen JJB, Dailey NS, Alkozei A, Vanuk JR & Killgore WDS (2024)**, "Improvements in
mindfulness, interoceptive and emotional awareness, emotion regulation, and interpersonal emotion
management following completion of an online emotional skills training program," *Emotion*
24(2):431–450, PMID 37535567. **Abstract read verbatim.**

**448 participants (323 female), RANDOMLY ASSIGNED to the training or a PLACEBO control.** Among
**N=326** completers: improved interoceptive and emotional awareness, mindfulness, **emotion
recognition**, and emotion-regulation strategies (reduced suppression, greater impulse control)
**relative to placebo.**

**Two things to hold against it.** **No effect sizes in the abstract** — full text needed before any
number is printed. And the 6-month result is weaker than it sounds: **N=94**, and verbatim
"sustained improvements were observed… **while the placebo group instead showed decreased
performance**," which the authors attribute to COVID. **So the follow-up effect may be the control
deteriorating rather than the trained group gaining.** By design this is now the strongest
trainability evidence in the project; by reporting it is incomplete.

**Also relevant and independent:** Bornemann & Singer 2017, *Psychophysiology* 54(3):469–482,
PMID 27925645 — objective **heartbeat perception accuracy** rose over 9 months of ReSource practice,
**d=.173 at 6 months, d=.273 at 9 months**, and change "was concomitant with and **predictive of**
changes in emotional awareness." Their own line: "**takes longer than the 8 weeks of typical
mindfulness courses to reach meaningful magnitude.**" **This is the honest ceiling: small, and slow.**

**C2. Awareness of your own emotions predicts reading others. `SUPPORTED — downgraded from
VALIDATED this audit`**
- **Israelashvili, Oosterwijk, Sauter & Fischer 2019**, *Cognition & Emotion* 33(7):1461–1471,
  PMID 30734635, DOI 10.1080/02699931.2019.1577221. **Abstract read verbatim. Study 1 N=363,
  Study 2 N=217, replicated** with tasks varying available emotional information. **No effect sizes
  in the abstract.**
- Grynberg 2012, 24 studies: face-reading deficits in alexithymia, and diagnostic-group differences
  vanished once alexithymia was controlled. **The authors state none of the included studies reported
  effect sizes.**
- **Why downgraded: Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak 2019**, *PSPI* 20(1):1–68, attacks
  the validity of the task both rest on. Reliability **r = .31**; the target facial configuration
  appeared in the matching episode **22% of the time (range .11–.35)**; specificity "largely
  unknown." Their recommendation: say **"a scowl," not "an expression of anger."**
- **Net:** the *individual-difference* claim is real and replicated; the *construct* it is measured
  with is contested. **Both must be stated together.** The lesson's safe form: people who know their
  own feelings tend to do better on emotion-recognition tasks — **and reading faces is less reliable
  than it feels**, which is the argument for asking.

**C3. LEAS carries a demographic confound. `VALIDATED — citation resolved this audit`**
**"Chuning 2025" is resolved: Chuning AE, Li N, Lane RD & Smith R (2025), "The Levels of Emotional
Awareness Scale (LEAS): Normative Data From a Community Sample and Observed Interactions With Sex,
Marital Status, Education, and Age," *Psychological Reports*, PMID 41170647,
DOI 10.1177/00332941251390433. Abstract read verbatim. n=381, M age 42.71, 51% female, 85.3% White.**
**Main effects of age, sex, education, SES, ethnicity and marital status** — the six predictors this
register claimed, now confirmed with a real citation. Higher scores for female, younger, more
educated, higher SES, white and single participants. Plus interactions sex×education,
sex×marital status, age×marital status. **Leverage: medium-high** — it means LEAS differences partly
index demography, which matters for any claim that a practice "raised awareness."

## 4. Tier D — the open question the lesson turns on

**D1. Interventions move *describing* and *externally-oriented thinking* but not *identifying*.
`CONTESTED — still the most important open question`**
For: Mazza 2026 meta, 47 studies, controlled — **DIF g=−0.06 ns, DDF −0.39, EOT −0.45.**

## Tier 0 — Measurement. Settle these first; they set the size of everything below.

### O6. Emotional expression — the closest coded analogue to affect labeling — is null against youth symptoms. And every protective association collapses longitudinally. `VALIDATED — full text read`
**Compas, Jaser, Bettis, Watson, Gruhn, Dunbar, Williams & Thigpen, 2017**, *Psychological
Bulletin* 143(9), 939–991 (**full text read**). **212 studies, N = 80,850**, ages 5–19,
published 2001–2012, **1,649 effect sizes**, 87 distinct measures. Random effects.
Cross-sectional and longitudinal reported separately. Egger's + trim-and-fill throughout.

**The single most relevant number in this literature for our premise:**
**Emotional expression × internalizing symptoms — r = .08, 95% CI [−.10, .25], k = 6,
non-significant.** Externalizing had only k=2, below their threshold, so it could not be
estimated. Their Appendix defines the emotional-expression category as: anger expression,
emotional expression, expressing emotions, expressing feelings, expression of emotions,
focusing and venting of emotions, physical release of emotions, **verbal sharing**. That is
affect labeling and expression as this field codes it, and across 80,850 youth it rests on
**six studies** and does not differ from zero.

**Strategy level — only 3 of 12 were significant, and all three are on the "don't" side:**

| Strategy | Internalizing | k | Externalizing | k |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Avoidance** | **.17** [.14,.20] (adj .16) | 65 | **.09** [.05,.13] | 32 |
| **Emotional suppression** | **.13** [.03,.22] *Egger sig* | 10 | .02 *ns* | 6 |
| **Denial** | **.10** [.03,.17] | 12 | — | 2 |
| Emotional expression | .08 *ns* | 6 | — | 2 |
| Cognitive reappraisal | −.05 *ns* | 31 | −.03 *ns* | 10 |
| Acceptance | .04 *ns* | 12 | — | 1 |
| Distraction | −.04 *ns* | 25 | .06 *ns* | 11 |
| Problem solving | −.03 *ns* | 45 | −.02 *ns* | 16 |

**Problem solving is null on 45 studies and 27,447 youth**, and reappraisal is null on 31.
Hold that against Boustani's finding that problem solving is in 76% of prevention programs
and takes 30% of their sessions.

**Factor level is where the signal lives** (aggregates, not single strategies):
secondary control coping **−.25** internalizing (adj −.20) / **−.30** externalizing (adj −.29);
primary control **−.17** (adj −.14) / **−.23**; disengagement **+.18** / **+.13**.
Engagement/approach was −.07 and **became non-significant after trim-and-fill (−.02)**.
Domain level: emotion regulation **−.23** internalizing / **−.27** externalizing (Egger
significant for both); adaptive coping −.11 externalizing; total coping **−.01, null**.

**The longitudinal collapse is the finding to carry forward.** Only **17** longitudinal studies.
Emotion regulation × internalizing **r = −.08, p = .52**. Primary control **−.07, p = .58**.
Secondary control **−.00, p = .90**. Engagement/approach null both outcomes. The only survivors
were **disengagement +.18**, **avoidance +.08** (which lost significance after trim-and-fill),
and — pointing the wrong way — **social support coping +.12, p<.001: more support-seeking,
*more* internalizing symptoms over time.**

Authors' own reading, which is the honest one: "it is possible that the only longitudinal
associations… are for those factors or strategies that are related to **higher** levels of
symptoms over time. In contrast, factors that are associated with **lower** levels of symptoms
(e.g., primary control coping and secondary control coping) **may only be correlates of
symptoms when measured at the same point in time.**"

**Their circularity warning applies to half the SEL literature too:** "findings on the use of
maladaptive coping are largely **tautological**. That is, if coping and emotion regulation are
labeled a priori as maladaptive or dysregulated then it is circular to show that these
strategies are related to higher levels of symptoms… we suggest that researchers **discontinue
the use of measures that label coping and emotion regulation as adaptive or maladaptive on an
a priori basis.**" Maladaptive coping's r = .27 — the largest domain effect in the paper — is
the one they disown.

**Two measurement facts that extend M1 beyond awareness into regulation:**
- **87 distinct measures across 212 studies.** Skinner 2003 counted **over 400 coping subtypes
  drawn from over 100 measures**; Adrian 2011 counted **100 emotion-regulation measures**
  between 1975 and 2010. Only **three** coping measures meet top-down CFA criteria (CCSC, RSQ,
  PRI) and only **three** ER measures report convergent/discriminant validity (DERS, ERQ, CEMS).
  **No emotion-regulation measure has factor-level scales at all.**
- **The DERS carries the same confound as the TAS-20:** "A concern with the DERS and other
  measures of emotion dysregulation is the considerable overlap with measures of
  psychopathology, particularly internalizing symptoms." Cross-reference [[M3]].

**What did *not* moderate anything:** measure quality — no significant moderation at any level.
Informant (single versus multi) — no significant moderation, which is genuine evidence the
associations are not merely shared method variance. Age produced only three effects, all
internalizing, all larger in adolescents than children (engagement/approach, cognitive
reappraisal, emotional suppression).

**Two scope limits to state whenever citing this:** the meta covers only **questionnaire**
measures — no EMA, no observation, no physiology — and **rumination was deliberately excluded**
because it blends automatic and controlled processes, even though Aldao 2010 found it the
single largest correlate of psychopathology. The largest-effect strategy in the adult
literature is outside this analysis by design.

### M1. "Emotional awareness" is at least four constructs, measured four ways, that do not agree with each other. `VALIDATED`
Self-report alexithymia (TAS-20), performance-based emotional awareness (LEAS),
behaviour-derived emotion differentiation (ICC from repeated ratings), and interoception
(which itself splits three ways).
**Evidence — Maroti, Lilliengren & Bileviciute-Ljungar 2018**, *Frontiers in Psychology*
(**full text read**). 21 studies, **28 independent samples, N=2,857**, six countries, mean
age 37, 57% female. Random effects. **r = −0.122** (95% CI [−0.180, −0.064], Z=−4.092,
p<0.001). Q=52.32 p=0.002, **I²=48.4%**. Trim-and-fill trimmed four studies and adjusted the
estimate down to **r = −0.092**.

**Three things in the forest plot that matter more than the pooled number:**
1. **The direction is not consistent.** Lundh 2002 (healthy) found **r = +0.240, p=0.034** —
   significant in the *opposite* direction. Waller & Scheidt 2004 (healthy) **+0.430, p=0.058**.
   Baeza-Velasco (medical) +0.224. Higher self-reported alexithymia went with *higher*
   measured awareness in those samples.
2. **Only 7 of 28 samples were individually significant in the expected direction**
   (Burger −0.249, Consoli −0.200, Lane 1998b −0.190, Maroti healthy −0.409, Pietri −0.410,
   Simson −0.173, Subic-Wrana 2001 −0.220). Twenty were null; one was significantly positive.
3. **Two of the three subgroups are not significantly different from zero.**
   Healthy controls r=−0.087, **p=0.051** (k=12, n=1,561); Medical r=−0.120, **p=0.095**
   (k=6, n=328); only Psychiatric reached significance, r=−0.130, p=0.021 (k=8, n=820).
   Between-group Q=0.595, p=0.743 — the subgroups don't differ from each other either.
   Age and gender were null moderators.

**A measurement inconsistency inside the meta:** several samples used **LEAS-10 rather than
LEAS-20** (Burger, Lane 2015a, Lichev, Maroti, Neumann, Simson, Subic-Wrana ×2, Waller), and
Waller & Scheidt appear to have divided their total by 10. Maroti did not moderate on this.

**Lundh 2002's individual-level finding, which the pooled r hides:** ~**20% reported low
alexithymia but performed worse than others on the LEAS**, and **15% reporting high alexithymia
performed better**. The relationship may be non-linear, not merely weak.

**The authors' own framing of what this contradicts:** the results "seem to contradict theories
of emotion that suggest that a person's ability to use words and concepts to label emotional
experience is related to their ability to experience and differentiate emotions (e.g., Barrett,
2006; Kashdan et al., 2015)." That is the constructionist premise underneath this whole lesson,
and the largest measurement study we have does not support it.

**Practical note for any practice we might build:** the 10-vignette LEAS takes participants
**up to 1.5 hours**, and a skilled rater needs 15–20 minutes to score it.

Differentiation
cross-method r≈.22 (Thompson 2021) and .10–.45 (Dunning 2025). Interoceptive accuracy,
sensibility and awareness dissociable (Garfinkel 2015). Emotional clarity and granularity
uncorrelated (Boden 2013, via Dunning 2025).
**Consequence:** no sentence in this lesson can say "emotional awareness" without naming
which measure. Most apparent contradictions in this research are two instruments
disagreeing, not two findings.

### M2. Self-report measures give moderate-to-large associations with outcomes; performance and behaviour-derived measures give small or null ones. `VALIDATED` (pattern) / `OPEN` (why)
**Evidence:** verbal side — TAS-20 r≈.32–.55 across outcomes vs. differentiation r=.06–.15
(O'Toole 2020, 27 studies) and r=−.15 (Seah & Coifman 2022). Non-verbal side —
self-reported interoception relates to anxiety, heartbeat-detection accuracy does not
(r=0.03, 40 studies, N=2,130).
**Two readings, unresolved:** (a) self-report is inflated by distress and the real effect
is small; (b) self-report captures something the tasks miss and the tasks were bad proxies.
**What would settle it:** LEAS-based outcome studies, since LEAS is performance-based *and*
uncorrelated with negative affect. See P6.

### M3. TAS-20 associations are substantially confounded with negative affect. `SUPPORTED — but not by Maroti`
**Maroti 2018 did not run this analysis.** Failing to use distress as a covariate is listed
in their **Limitations**, where they summarise other people's work: associations between LEAS
and population were "not altered by removing variance due to negative affect"
(Bydlowski 2005; Subic-Wrana 2005; Consoli 2009), whereas "control for negative affect made
associations with the TAS-20 non-significant." Those three primary studies are the actual
evidence and **none has been read.**

**The strongest single result is Leising, Grande & Faber 2009**, whose title states the claim:
"The Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20): a measure of general psychological distress." Raters
blind to TAS-20 scores coded videotaped interviews for number of emotions expressed. Patients
who rated themselves as having *more* difficulty describing emotions were **better** at
expressing them than others — **not worse**. Via Maroti; not read.

Lundh 2002 found TAS-20 correlated highly with **perfectionism**. Preece 2020 and Marchesi 2014
argue the difficulty-identifying-feelings facet tracks general distress.

**Consequence unchanged, attribution corrected:** the confound is real and multiply reported,
but cite Leising 2009 and the three NA-control studies, never Maroti, for it.

### M4. LEAS avoids the distress confound but carries a verbal-fluency one — and a demographic one that is worse. `VALIDATED`
**Evidence:** word-count correlations with LEAS range from zero (Lane 1990) to r=0.48–0.71
(Barchard & Picker 2018). Verbal skill significantly predicted LEAS-C in children.
**Note:** the authors' defence — that controlling word count "removes substantive variance"
— is a theoretical argument, not a result. Their empirical support is that partial
correlations survive.

**Escalated 13 Aug 2026 — the demographic loading is the bigger problem.**
**Chuning, Li, Lane & Smith, 2025**, *Psychological Reports*, normative community sample
(`secondary source` — search result, **primary text not read**): main effects on LEAS scores of
**age, sex, education level, SES, ethnicity, and marital status.** Higher scores for
**female, younger, more educated, higher-SES, white, and single** participants.

**Why this is worse than word count.** The LEAS is a **written elaboration task** scored for the
complexity of what you produce. Education and SES predicting performance on it is the
verbal-fluency confound with a demographic gradient attached. Ethnicity predicting performance on
a written task about emotion scenarios looks like a language-and-culture artifact rather than a
difference in emotional capacity. **Six demographic variables predicting the score means a
person's rung is substantially a function of who they are, not only of what they notice.**

**Consequence, strengthened:** neither flagship instrument is clean. Any LEAS-based claim in this
register — including the whole of [[I2]], which is the interpersonal cluster's strongest-looking
leg — inherits this. Say so rather than picking one.

### M6. The low rungs of the LEAS mark a specific presentation — distress as body rather than as named feeling — not general impairment. `SUPPORTED — secondary, primary texts not read`
**The rungs are bandable.** The 20-item LEAS has a maximum of **100**, and the bands are explicit:
**0–20 = level 1, 81–100 = level 5**, dividing evenly between. So a total score does place a person
on a rung, and asking what is associated with a rung is a well-posed question. *(This register
previously implied the levels were only a scoring rubric with no ordinal reading. Wrong —
Darryl's correction, 13 Aug 2026.)*

**The five rungs:** 0 = non-emotion · 1 = physiological cues · 2 = action tendencies ·
3 = a single specific emotion · 4 = two or more differentiated emotions ·
5 = two or more differentiated emotions for two or more persons.

**Where people actually sit — from Maroti's Table 1 (full text read):** healthy and clinical
samples alike cluster at **levels 3–4**. University students 50.0 (L3); healthy adults 62.7 and
68.3 (L4); anorexia patients **62.8 versus their own controls 62.4 — identical, both L4**; eating
disorders 61.0 vs controls 66.4; essential hypertension 46.2 (L3). **Almost no sample sits at
levels 1–2.** This bears on the working premise: on this instrument most adults are at
single-emotion or blends, not at the preverbal rungs.

**The one genuine rung-outcome association (Subic-Wrana 2010, secondary):** patients with
**somatoform disorders scored at the implicit levels — 1 and 2 — while patients with eating
disorders or depression, and healthy controls, scored significantly higher.**

**Why this is the useful form of the claim.** It is not "low awareness is bad." It is that the low
rungs track a **particular presentation**: distress arriving as bodily symptom rather than as named
feeling. Depression and eating disorders do **not** sit there. Mechanistically coherent — operating
at sensation and action tendency without the emotion label leaves the body as the only channel —
and it is the cleanest support for the alexithymia–somatisation hypothesis in this review.

**Consequences for the lesson.**
1. **The ladder is not worth teaching as a ladder.** No level-specific predictive validity exists,
   level 5 is partly an artifact of self-versus-other differentiation, and per [[M4]] the rung is
   predicted by six demographic variables including education, SES and ethnicity.
2. **There is one defensible use:** the low rungs identify who the interoceptive and
   sensation-focused work is *for* — people whose distress presents somatically. That gives the
   interoception evidence (g=0.31, 29 RCTs, plus one objective-accuracy trial) a population
   instead of a general recommendation.
3. **Lane & Schwartz cannot be cited as support for teaching levels 1–2 as a destination.** It is a
   **cognitive-developmental** model in the Piagetian mould: higher is later and better, and 1–2 are
   the rungs children occupy before developing further. The model supplies the vocabulary for
   "awareness with or without naming"; it does not endorse it. The support for the low rungs
   mattering comes from elsewhere — interoception trainability, Kircanski (body moved d=0.64–0.85
   while self-reported fear did not), Hamilton 2025 (affect variability predicted, granularity did
   not).

**Not yet checked:** whether any study reports outcomes by rung *within* a diagnosis, or tracks
people moving between rungs. **Retrieval:** Subic-Wrana 2010 (somatoform), Chuning 2025 (norms).

### M5. The seven-category vocabulary is not arbitrary — it predicts the Big Five. `SUPPORTED — supplied by Darryl 13 Aug 2026, primary text not read, no effect sizes`
**Why this is a Tier 0 entry:** the choice of which emotion words to teach is a measurement
decision before it is a pedagogical one. If we hand a reader seven categories, the question is
whether those categories carve at joints.

**The argument.** The Big Five (Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness,
Neuroticism) is the most widely accepted model of human personality, but it is **purely
descriptive and lexical** — derived by statistically categorising descriptive words in the
dictionary, not by examining the brain. **Panksepp's seven primary emotional systems**
(SEEKING, RAGE/ANGER, FEAR, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, PLAY) were mapped by electrical
stimulation of the mammalian brain, pharmacological challenge and lesion work.

**A 2021 *Scientific Reports* meta-analysis** tested whether the primary emotional systems,
measured with the **Affective Neuroscience Personality Scales (ANPS)**, predict the Big Five.
Reported mappings:

| Big Five trait | ANPS primary emotional system |
|---|---|
| Openness to Experience | strongly predicted by high **SEEKING** |
| Extraversion | strongly predicted by high **PLAY** |
| Agreeableness | high **CARE** and low **ANGER** |
| Neuroticism | high **FEAR**, **SADNESS**, and **ANGER** |
| Conscientiousness | *no primary-emotion predictor listed* |

**What this buys the lesson.** Seven names is defensible as a *sufficient working vocabulary*
rather than a simplification, because the categories predict the taxonomy people already
recognise. And the direction of the argument runs the useful way: from the biologically-derived
set to the lexically-derived one, not the reverse.

**Four things to hold before leaning on it:**

1. **No effect sizes.** "Strongly predicted" and "predicted" are not numbers, and this is a
   meta-analysis — it has them. **Retrieval priority: the actual correlations.** Nothing here may
   cite a direction without a magnitude, per the five-coordinates rule.
2. **This is questionnaire-to-questionnaire, and [[M2]] is the warning.** The subcortical circuits
   were mapped by stimulation in animals; the **ANPS is self-report**, as is the Big Five. Two
   self-reports correlating is the weak form of evidence — precisely the pattern where this
   literature has inflated associations throughout. The mapping shows the ANPS *scales* behave
   coherently. It does not establish that a respondent's ANPS score indexes their subcortical
   circuitry. Darryl has stated he is not claiming biological accuracy, only sufficiency of the
   vocabulary; this register should not quietly upgrade that.
3. **The seven are not orthogonal.** Three systems (FEAR, SADNESS, ANGER) load on Neuroticism
   alone, and ANGER appears twice — negatively on Agreeableness, positively on Neuroticism. This
   is not a clean seven-to-five mapping, and teaching the seven as cleanly separable would
   overstate it.
4. **LUST has no mapping in the table, and Conscientiousness has no predictor.** The
   Conscientiousness gap fits the theory — conscientiousness is executive and cortical rather than
   affective — but it should be said rather than left as an omission. LUST's absence is separate,
   and is also the category a curriculum will have to make a decision about.

**Relevance to [[O1]]:** the set is **positive-majority** — four positive systems (SEEKING, LUST,
CARE, PLAY) against three negative (FEAR, RAGE, PANIC/GRIEF). Nearly every emotion vocabulary in
this review is negative-heavy, and positive-emotion awareness is a standing gap because it is
under-studied. A majority-positive vocabulary may be an advantage rather than a compromise.

**Distinct from the granularity deflation, and this matters.** [[O'Toole 2020]]'s r=.06–.15 and
Nook's vocabulary confound concern **within-category** discrimination — how many words you have
for the negative-activated quadrant. Pond 2012 was finer *anger* distinctions specifically. The
seven are **across-category**. The deflation evidence does not straightforwardly apply to a
seven-category scheme, and nothing we have read tests 7 versus 20 versus 50.

**The stronger support for this vocabulary is [[P4d]], not the Big Five mapping above.** Torre
2016 experimentally varied abstraction and intensity independently and found **basic emotion
words ("angry") produced larger amygdala reductions than fine-grained ones ("irritated,"
"enraged"), while intensity did nothing.** That tests the *act of labeling with words at this
level*, rather than whether the ANPS scales cohere with another self-report instrument. If M5
has to rest on one leg, it should be that one.

## Tier 1 — Q1: outcomes associated with awareness

### O1. Positive-emotion differentiation is null **for the outcomes the negative-differentiation studies tested**, and has a separate literature with different outcomes that this project has not reviewed. `OPEN`
**What is actually established:** when a study set out to test negative-emotion
differentiation and measured positive as a comparison, positive came out null — regulation
frequency (Barrett 2001), depression diagnosis (Demiralp 2012), social anxiety diagnosis
(Kashdan & Farmer 2014), well-being variables in 14-year-olds (Lennarz 2018), change under
6 weeks of experience sampling (Widdershoven 2019), change under 8-week MBSR (Guendelman
2025).
**What the founding paper of the positive line actually shows — read in full, and it is
thin.** Tugade, Fredrickson & Feldman Barrett, **2004**, "Psychological Resilience and
Positive Emotional Granularity," *Journal of Personality* 72:1161–1190 (**full text read**).
Two studies, and they are not what the title implies:
- **Study 1 does not measure granularity at all.** N=57, speech-preparation stressor. Trait
  resilience (self-report) → faster cardiovascular recovery (r=−.26, p<.05), mediated by
  positive emotionality. Granularity is absent.
- **Study 2** is the granularity study. N=130, 28 days, 10 prompts/day, positive granularity
  as the ICC across five positive emotion words (amusement, happiness, interest, joy,
  pride). **The outcome is a self-report coping questionnaire. No health outcome, no
  wellbeing outcome, no symptom outcome was measured.**
- **Of 18 correlations reported, four reached significance**, all r=.18–.26: less mental
  self-distraction (−.26), **more behavioural disengagement (+.20)**, and less
  experiential/automatic thinking style (−.18, −.18). Acceptance, active coping, denial,
  emotional support, humour, instrumental support, planning, positive reframing, religion,
  self-blame, substance use and venting were all null.
- The one positive-signed significant finding, behavioural disengagement, sits on a COPE
  scale conventionally treated as **maladaptive**; the authors read it generously as
  "taking a momentary pause."
- Authors' own limitation: positive granularity "may be most effective in low-stress
  contexts, where there is no immediate press to respond with quick and decisive action."

**Other positive-granularity findings located but not yet read:** low positive
differentiation in anorexia and weight-loss activity (Selby 2014); lower positive
granularity with greater odds of binge eating (Mikhail 2020); both valences related to
lower stress, lower rumination and higher self-esteem (Erbas 2021); positive granularity
rose *more* than negative under intensive sampling, d=0.50 vs 0.32 (Hoemann 2021).

**Correct statement:** the negative-differentiation studies' positive-valence nulls were
tested against outcomes chosen for negative emotions, so they do not establish that
positive awareness is inert. But the founding paper of the positive line is one ESM study
whose outcomes are self-reported coping style, with four significant correlations under
r=.27 and no health measure. **Neither "positive awareness doesn't matter" nor "positive
awareness has its own strong literature" is supportable right now.**

### O2. Effect sizes shrink from clinical to general to student populations. `VALIDATED`
**Evidence:** Honkalampi 2022 substance use d=0.83 clinical / 0.51 general / 0.28 students.
Alimoradi 2022 sleep r=.55 patients / .30 healthy.
**Consequence:** name direction, never magnitude, when writing for a general reader.

### O3. People who are more aware of their own emotions read other people *better*, not worse. `VALIDATED`
**Evidence:** Israelashvili 2019 (two studies, N=363 and 217); Grynberg 2012 (24 studies,
and diagnostic-group differences vanished once alexithymia was controlled); LEAS studies on
face, scene and text recognition (Lane 1990, 1996, 2000b; Wright 2017).
**Also debunks** the folk belief that being out of touch with yourself sharpens you about
others.

### O4. More awareness is not monotonically better. `SUPPORTED`
**Evidence:** Lane 2021 states it outright. Higher emotional awareness went with *greater*
anxiety in generalized anxiety disorder (Novick-Kline 2005). Higher female LEAS predicted
lower marital quality in 56 couples, reversing when partners' scores converged (Croyle &
Waltz 2002). Erbas 2018 authors argue collapsing distinctions under stress may be adaptive
simplification rather than failure.
**Consequence:** the lesson cannot promise a monotonic dose-response on awareness itself.

### O5. Anxiety is the strongest association in the alexithymia literature. `CONTESTED`
**For:** Yeung 2026, SMD=0.97 vs controls, r=.39 severity, unusually low heterogeneity
(I²=21.5%).
**Against:** the anxiety data is overwhelmingly panic disorder (12 of ~21 groups; 4 social,
2 GAD), and narrowed to panic alone drops to 0.77. Generalized anxiety disorder is a
documented exception twice — unrelated to lower differentiation (Decker 2008, via Thompson
2021), and *positively* related to awareness (Novick-Kline 2005). In adolescents the
depression link is better evidenced than the anxiety one: 10 studies vs 2 (Dunning 2025).
**Was over-claimed early in this project.** Treat as "anxiety is where the largest clinical
group differences are, in panic disorder specifically."

### I2. Difficulty identifying and describing feelings goes with less social support and more reluctance to seek help. `SUPPORTED — four verified sources converging; the original four citations were chased 18 Aug 2026 and none supports it`

- **Zhang X and colleagues, 2025** (abstract read verbatim) — **N = 430**, men after radical

### I3. Alexithymia relates to lower relationship and sexual satisfaction. `SUPPORTED — downgraded from VALIDATED`
**Humphreys, Wood & Parker, 2009**, *Personality and Individual Differences* 46:43–47
(**full text read**). This was the load-bearing leg and it is smaller than the register implied:
**N = 158 undergraduates** at one Ontario university — **34 men, 124 women**, mean age **19.4**,
88% White, all in heterosexual relationships of **at least 3 months** (mean length **17.6
months**). The authors call it "one of the first studies to explore" this link.

**Two numbers, and the register was quoting the larger one.** Observed correlations:
TAS-20 × relationship satisfaction **r = −.45**, TAS-20 × sexual satisfaction **r = −.44**.
The **−0.52 and −0.51** figures are **latent-variable SEM paths**, which are disattenuated for
measurement error and therefore always larger than raw correlations. Model fit: RMSR 0.051,
GFI 0.893, AGFI 0.826, CFI 0.955.

**The old "−.45 to −.55 across four samples" phrasing must not be used** — it pooled Humphreys's
latent βs with Besharat's raw *r*s. Those are different quantities. State it as: raw
correlations around **−.44 to −.49** where reported.

**The negative-affect control is genuine but weaker than it sounds.** PANAS-negative correlated
with TAS-20 at r = .28, but with relationship satisfaction only −.15 (ns) and sexual
satisfaction −.14 (ns); both SEM paths were .02 and non-significant. So mood did not explain the
association — a real strength. **But the PANAS here measured affective state "at the time they
were completing the questionnaires."** That is *momentary mood over ~20 minutes*, **not trait
negative affectivity**, which is the actual confound at issue in [[M3]]. This does not clear the
trait-NA objection; it clears a much smaller one.

**Design limits, all of them fatal to a VALIDATED tag:** cross-sectional; **every variable is
self-report from the same person** (own alexithymia, own relationship satisfaction, own sexual
satisfaction — no partner report, shared method variance unaddressed); undergraduates in short
relationships; only 34 men, so the authors could not analyse by gender. Their own limitation
section asks for replication in long-term relationships and in dyads.

**One finding here that cuts against our own summary.** By subscale, **difficulty *describing*
feelings is the strongest correlate** (DDF −.43 relationship / −.42 sexual), ahead of difficulty
*identifying* feelings (DIF −.34 / −.29) and externally oriented thinking (EOT −.29 / −.31).
If the verbal facet carries more of the interpersonal association than the identifying facet,
that is a point **for** the naming side of the premise in this one domain — and it is the only
place in the whole review where that has happened. Worth checking whether Besharat and Panahi
replicate the subscale ordering.

**Still unread:** Besharat 2014 (N=688 married Iranian students, r=−.49 women, −.45 men) and
Panahi 2018 (108 Iranian couples), whose reported mechanism finding — direct path
non-significant, whole effect running through **loneliness** — remains `secondary source`.
Candidate local files: `The_Relationship_Between_Alexithymia_and_Marital_S.pdf`,
`Formulating_a_model_for_the_relationship_between_a.pdf`.

### I4. Expressing emotion helps social outcomes; suppressing hurts. The anger exception is probably about *aggressive* expression, not expression as such. `SUPPORTED — needs the PDF; the anger finding is not usable as stated`
Chervonsky & Hunt, **2017**, *Emotion*, 43 papers, 105 effect sizes (`secondary source`,
paywalled, **primary text not read**). Suppression → poorer social wellbeing (first impressions,
social support, satisfaction, romantic quality). Expression of **positive and general** emotion
→ better outcomes. Expression of **anger** → worse. Expression of negative emotion generally →
worse, but very small and mixed.

**Correction, 13 Aug 2026 (Darryl's catch).** This register previously carried the anger result as
"expressing anger hurts," and I used it in conversation to rule out voicing anger as a route to
boundary-setting. **That over-read an unread source.** We do not know how anger expression was
operationalised across the 43 papers, and the forms are not interchangeable — yelling, venting,
hostile expression and calmly stating a boundary have no reason to share an outcome.

**Why the aggressive reading is the likely one.** The **STAXI**, the dominant anger instrument,
defines **Anger Expression-Out** as "how often angry feelings are expressed in verbally or
physically **aggressive** behavior" — aggression is in the operational definition. Assertive
expression is filed separately under **Anger Control-Out**, and the STAXI's own intervention
logic prescribes **assertiveness training as the treatment for high Anger-In** (chronic
suppression). So in the field's own framework, assertive boundary-setting is not an instance of
the harmful category; it is the remedy for the opposite problem.

**The internal contradiction that should have caught this earlier.** [[P17a]] records
**assertiveness training in 45% of Boustani's 58 prevention programs** — tied with communication
skills, ahead of insight building. Assertive boundary-setting *is* calm anger expression. If
Chervonsky & Hunt's category captured it, the field would be running a harmful practice in nearly
half its programs without noticing.

**Related distinctions the literature already makes**, none yet verified here: constructive versus
destructive anger expression (Davidson and colleagues, anger and blood pressure); and the
**venting** literature, where catharsis *increases* aggression rather than discharging it. Form of
expression is a recognised moderator, not an afterthought.

**Status of the usable claim:** suppression → worse social outcomes, and positive/general
expression → better, both stand pending the PDF. **The anger result may only license
"aggressively expressed anger hurts," which does not constrain the lesson.** Getting the
operationalisation is now the specific reason this PDF is a priority — not the effect size.

**Two more in the same shape, both unread:**
- **Yelsma & Marrow, 2003** — discrepancies between husbands' and wives' difficulties with
  emotional expressiveness impaired **both their own and their spouses'** marital satisfaction.
  (Via Humphreys 2009's citation.)
- **Frye-Cox & Hesse, 2013**, *Journal of Family Psychology* 27(2):203–211 — **N=155 couples,
  dyadic design**; loneliness and intimate communication **fully mediated** the
  alexithymia–marital-quality link.

**Why this is high leverage.** It is the only mechanism we have that would make emotional
vocabulary **conditionally** useful rather than uniformly good — helpful when matched, harmful
when mismatched. If it holds, it changes what we can honestly tell a reader about naming
feelings to another person: the instruction is not "say how you feel," it is something closer to
"say how you feel to someone who can meet you there." It also gives a mechanism for [[O4]]
(awareness is not monotonically better) that isn't just a ceiling effect.

**Why it can't be built on yet.** 56 couples, cross-sectional, primary text unread, LEAS-based
so subject to [[M4]]'s verbal-fluency confound, and no replication located. **Croyle & Waltz is
now a top-three retrieval priority alongside Killgore 2020 and Chervonsky & Hunt.**

**On the direction:** the *high*-awareness women were the ones with lower relationship quality.
This is not an argument against building awareness. It is what mismatch means — a relationship
where one person can express emotion and the other cannot will have problems, and the partner who
notices the gap is the one who reports it. Awareness is revealing a deficit in the relationship,
not creating one. The interesting part of the study is not the main effect but **the reversal on
convergence.**

### I5. Awareness of one's own emotions predicts accuracy about others — but narrowly for a partner. `SUPPORTED`
Israelashvili 2019 (N=363, 217) and Grynberg 2012 (24 studies; group differences vanished
controlling alexithymia) establish direction; Grynberg reports **no effect sizes at all**.
LEAS studies converge (faces, scenes, text).
**The partner-specific limit:** Erbas 2016, 49 Belgian couples, 7 days — differentiation
correlated with accuracy about a partner's **valence** at r=.30, but was **null for arousal
accuracy and null for tracking a partner's mood changes over time.**

### I6. Emotional intelligence and romantic relationship satisfaction correlate at r=.373. `SUPPORTED — needs the PDF`

### I7. Low differentiation plus low self-esteem predicts a stronger neural response to social rejection. `SUPPORTED, SMALL`

## Tier 2 — Q2: practices that shift it

### P1. Repeatedly rating specific emotions moves negative-emotion differentiation. `SUPPORTED`
**Evidence:** Widdershoven 2019 — 6 weeks, 3 days/week, 10×/day, 55 adults with diagnosed
depression vs 24 no-ESM controls: negative differentiation d=0.43, group effect p=.015;
positive ns. Hoemann 2021 — ~14 days, 50 healthy adults, uncontrolled: negative d=0.32,
positive d=0.50.
**Weakness:** two studies; one has a control group, one doesn't.

### P2. Learning emotion vocabulary moves differentiation. `OPEN`
**For:** Vedernikova 2021, 5 days self-directed online, N=120, η²p=0.174, still present at
one month.
**Against:** Matt 2024, N=118, similar design, did not beat neutral control on
differentiation (p=.174) — though among higher-engagement participants, post-task
differentiation predicted lower distress a week later.
**Reads as a dose/engagement effect rather than a failure of the idea, but that is a
reading.**

### P3. More practice per day does not produce more skill. `SUPPORTED BUT FRAGILE`
**Evidence:** four dose nulls — sessions in Mazza 2026 (p=.51); dosage, attendance and
duration in the 2025 interoception meta-analysis; number of reports in Widdershoven 2019
(p=.117). Plus one *negative* finding: more prompts per day predicted **lower** negative
granularity in Hoemann 2021 (β=−0.53).
**Weakness:** three of five are nulls, and Widdershoven's range was restricted (everyone
≥87 reports). What did matter, for writing, was spacing: 1–3 day intervals beat longer
(Guo 2023).

### P4. Benefit is delayed, and may be preceded by feeling worse. `SUPPORTED`
**Evidence, two independent literatures:** expressive writing effects emerged at follow-up
not post-test, with short-term effects null-to-negative (Guo 2023, 31 RCTs, N=4,012). And
self-generated affect labels produce delayed rather than immediate effects, with possible
transient distress increase (Torre & Lieberman 2018), consistent with no immediate
self-report benefit in the two real-exposure studies (Kircanski 2012, Niles 2015).

### P4b. Handing someone a set of emotion words works differently from asking them to come up with their own. `SUPPORTED — primary text read`
**Torre, J. (2016)**, *How Putting Feelings into Words Reduces Our Emotional Experiences:
Understanding Mechanisms of Affect Labeling*, PhD dissertation, UCLA (Lieberman, chair).
**Full text read 13 Aug 2026.** The *Emotion Review* 2018 paper (Torre & Lieberman, 10(2),
116–124) is this document's background chapter; the dissertation additionally reports three
original fMRI studies testing the claim. Cite the dissertation for the experiments.

## The observation, stated verbatim by the author

> "One feature many of these studies showing delayed effects have in common is that in most
> cases (cf. Tabibnia et al., 2008), the task protocol required participants to **generate affect
> labels themselves rather than have them be provided**… In fact, the only cases where affect
> labeling significantly **increased** self-reported affect or autonomic arousal during the
> initial exposure also adopted a paradigm that required participants to **self-generate and
> verbalize** their emotional experiences (Mendolia & Kleck, 1993; Ortner, 2015)."

And on expressive writing, which is self-generation with almost no scaffolding: long-term
benefits months later, no immediate benefit, and "many have reported **feeling more negative
affect immediately after writing sessions**" (Baikie & Wilhelm 2005; Esterling et al. 1999).

## The experiment — Study 3, "Number of Responses task"

**N=20** healthy right-handed adults (23 recruited, 3 excluded), 17 female, mean age 21.48,
UCLA and surrounding community, fMRI, aversive IAPS images, four conditions matched on
valence and arousal (all ns):
- **One Label** — evaluate whether one provided word fits your reaction
- **Two Labels** — choose between two provided words (the standard paradigm)
- **Free Response** — generate your own label, press a button when decided
- **Observe** — attend and let emotions respond naturally

**Results (amygdala ROI, parameter estimates vs. Observe):**
- **One Label > Observe: significant bilateral reductions in *every* subregion.**
- **Two Labels > Observe: bilateral decreases**, same pattern.
- **Free Response > Observe: only the left superficial amygdala** (t=2.643, p=.008). Everything
  else null.
- **Provided Labels (One + Two) > Free Response** — right amygdala more deactivated by provided
  labels: **laterobasal t=2.930 p=.004; whole-amygdala AAL t=2.497 p=.011; centromedial t=2.150
  p=.023.** Plus right vlPFC (AL-OBS peak) t=2.112 p=.024.

All three conditions raised vlPFC and dlPFC over Observe — **the prefrontal machinery came
online for self-generation too. It just didn't succeed in bringing the amygdala down.** Torre
predicted exactly this in advance: vlPFC "brought online to regulate amygdala but is disrupted
or otherwise unsuccessful."

## The caveat that limits how far this generalises — and it is large

**The Free Response condition was not free.** Participants were "instructed before the study to
mentally choose from the same words listed above" — angry, scared, sad, disgusted, happy — in
order to eliminate individual differences in granularity. So the contrast is really *shown the
word* versus *retrieve it from a five-word set you were given*, not versus open generation.
Torre flags it himself: "it remains to be seen how successful affect labeling would (or would
not) be when participants are not constrained in such a manner."

**If a five-word retrieval is already enough to blunt the effect, open generation is unlikely to
do better** — but that is an inference, not a result.

**Other limits, all stated by the author:** N=20 is "quite low… leaving us underpowered to
obtain reliable true effects"; all three tasks run on the same participants in one session
against a structure known for rapid habituation; low ecological validity (images of events
distant from oneself); and **outcomes are immediate amygdala activity only** — the delayed
benefit that motivates the whole question was not measured here.

## What this settles and what it doesn't

**Settles:** the form of the instruction changes the immediate neural result, in the direction
claimed. Provided beats self-generated *in the moment*.

**Does not settle:** whether self-generation buys the delayed benefit. That comes from a
separate set of studies (Kircanski 2012; Mendolia & Kleck 1993; Niles 2015; Tabibnia 2008) and
**the trade-off has never been tested within one experiment.** Nobody has run provided vs.
self-generated with a one-week follow-up. That is now the single cleanest open question in
Tier 2.

## The mechanism Torre proposes, which links to [[P4c]]

Provided labels may work because being handed the word by someone else is a form of
**interpersonal emotion regulation** (Zaki & Williams 2013) — external support — and "in some
cases, emotional regulation can be more easily initiated with support from others." **He cites
Nils & Rimé 2012 for this**, the same paper that gives us the socio-affective/cognitive sharing
distinction. The two claims are not independent; they may be one mechanism.

Alternative accounts he offers: self-generation demands broader self-monitoring, or
verbalising internal states under observation raises self-consciousness. Also possible that the
delayed effect is specific to **anxiety-disorder populations**, since most delayed-effect studies
were clinical.

**Converging evidence already in the register:** Guo 2023 (31 RCTs, N=4,012) found
expressive-writing effects at follow-up, not post-test. Kircanski 2012 and Niles 2015 found no
immediate self-report benefit in real exposure. Same shape, four sources.

### P4d. Basic emotion words beat fine-grained ones. Abstraction is what matters, not intensity. `SUPPORTED — primary text read, N=20`
**Torre 2016 dissertation, Study 1, "Label Intensity task."** Same N=20 sample. NimStim facial
expressions (angry, fearful, happy), four conditions, words pre-rated for intensity 1–7 by a
separate 57-person MTurk sample:
- **Abstract** — *angry* (5.54), *scared* (5.37), *happy* (4.60)
- **Low Intensity** — *frustrated* (4.40), *irritated* (3.86), *startled* (4.39), *worried* (4.23)
- **High Intensity** — *enraged* (6.81), *furious* (6.63), *terrified* (6.68), *horrified* (6.39)
- **Gender** — control (choose the gendered name)

The abstract words sit **mid-scale between** low and high, so abstraction is not confounded with
intensity. That design choice is what makes the result usable.

**Result, in the author's words:** "Using more abstract emotion words (such as **'angry' rather
than 'irritated' or 'enraged'**) yields larger decreases in amygdala activity when used in an
affect labeling paradigm **while varying the intensity of the words did not.**"

**Specific > Abstract** — the fine-grained words produced *more* amygdala activity: left
laterobasal **t=2.590 p=.009**, left whole-amygdala AAL **t=1.910 p=.036**, right centromedial
p=.091 (trending).

**Intensity failed in the predicted direction.** Low > High produced *more* right amygdala
activity (superficial t=2.058 p=.028) — the opposite of the hypothesis that gentler words
would regulate better. Whole-brain: "No results to report" for High > Low.

## Why this matters for the lesson

This is the **granularity question tested at the neural level, and it favours fewer, coarser
words.** Every other granularity result in this register is correlational
([[O'Toole 2020]] r=.06–.15; Nook's vocabulary confound). This one is experimental, and it says
a basic-level category regulates better than a precise shade of the same feeling.

**It is the strongest direct support [[M5]]'s seven-category vocabulary has** — better than the
ANPS/Big-Five mapping, because it tests the *act of labeling with those words* rather than the
coherence of the scales. Panksepp's seven are basic-level categories in exactly the sense
"angry" is and "enraged" is not.

**Do not overstate it.** N=20, underpowered by the author's own statement, immediate amygdala
activity only, faces-in-a-scanner, and the vlPFC pattern did **not** mirror the amygdala as the
regulatory account predicts — bilateral vlPFC and dlPFC were *more* active for Specific. Torre's
own verdict: "given the strange and unexpected vlPFC findings, the true story is undoubtedly
more complicated." Nothing here tests 7 versus 20 versus 50 words, and nothing tests retention
or behaviour.

**Also from Study 2 (Types of Affect Labeling), same sample:** labeling **affect** (own or
others') beat labeling **aversive content** — left laterobasal amygdala less active (p=.035),
bilateral vlPFC more active. This **reverses** McRae, Taitano & Lane 2010, which found objective
content labels reduced skin conductance more than subjective ones. And **labeling your own
emotions versus someone else's showed no difference** — "Internal Affect > External Affect: No
results to report."

### P4c. Venting and social sharing — **MOVED to Lesson 25 (Talk About It)** and Lesson 8, 13 Aug 2026

Nils & Rimé 2012, Lepore 2004, Bushman 2002 and the partner-convergence cluster (Croyle & Waltz,
Yelsma & Marrow, Frye-Cox & Hesse, the I3 subscale ordering, Chervonsky & Hunt) are claims about
**talking to someone**, not about noticing what you feel. Write-ups now in

**One finding stays here, because it is about self-knowledge rather than about talking.** People in
the empathic-listener condition reported at 48 hours that the conversation had made them feel
better, changed their perspective, and reduced their negative feelings. **None of the three had
happened.** They got closeness and misread it as recovery.

Together with two others, that makes a pattern this lesson owns:

| Study | People believed | What actually happened |
|---|---|---|
| Nils & Rimé 2012 | the talk helped, reframed, and calmed them | none of the three |
| Torre 2016 | labeling would make them feel **worse** | it reduced amygdala response |
| Schweiger Gallo 2009 | goal-intention group thought they'd done **better** (6.61 vs 5.22) | they had done nothing; the plan group improved |

**Three independent demonstrations that people cannot introspect on whether an emotional
intervention worked.** That is a claim about emotional awareness, it points in an uncomfortable
direction for this lesson, and it is one of the better-supported things in this file.

### P20b. The pause — taught everywhere, isolated almost nowhere. `OPEN — searched 13 Aug 2026` 

**SCOPE FLAG, 13 Aug 2026 — left in place deliberately, needs Darryl's call.** The pause spans at
least three other lessons: **3 (Observation)** as monitoring/acceptance, **19 (Overriding)** as the
gap between stimulus and response, and **21 (Distress Tolerance)** as urge surfing. It is not
obviously a Lesson 2 claim — noticing a feeling and pausing before acting on it are different moves.
Not moved because the right destination is a design decision, not a filing one.
**Scope note, and it is the point of this entry.** A first search restricted to SEL programs and
folk anger advice found almost nothing and I nearly recorded the pause as untested. **That was a
search failure, not a finding.** The pause is the core move of mindfulness practice, of
Frankl's space between stimulus and response, and of the Buddhist sacred pause; it is a measured
facet of the standard mindfulness instrument; it is *urge surfing* in addiction treatment; and
its installation mechanism is *implementation intentions* in goal psychology. Almost none of
that evidence sits in the emotion-education literature this project had been reading.

## 1. As a trait: non-reactivity is measured and it correlates. `correlational`
The **FFMQ** five facets are Observing, Describing, **Acting with Awareness**, Non-judging, and
**Non-reacting to inner experience** ("When I have distressing thoughts or images, I just notice
them and let them go"). Two meta-analyses:
- **Affective symptoms:** Nonjudge **r=−0.48**, **Act with Awareness r=−0.47**,
  **Nonreact r=−0.33**, Describe **r=−0.29**.
- **Life satisfaction:** Acting-with-Awareness **0.35**, Describing **0.31**,
  Non-reacting **0.28**.

**Two things to notice.** *Describing* — the labeling facet — is the **weakest of the four** on
affective symptoms. That is the same ordering [[O6]], [[P4c]] and [[P17]] keep producing.
And **Observing does not appear in either list**, which fits its known behaviour as the facet
that correlates the wrong way in non-meditators.

**Discount appropriately:** both sides are self-report, which is exactly the inflation pattern
[[M2]] warns about. These are associations, not effects.

## 2. As an installable mechanism: implementation intentions. `experimental — the strongest leg`
If-then plans — "**if** situation X occurs, **then** I will do Y" — are the most direct
operationalisation of a taught pause, and the literature is large:
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006:** 94 independent tests, **d = 0.65**.
- **2024 meta-analysis: 642 independent tests**, effective for **cognitive, affective and
  behavioural** outcomes, effect sizes **.27 to .66**.
- **Anger regulation is a named domain** in that meta-analytic list.
- **Mental Contrasting with Implementation Intentions for anger** (Frontiers 2018, two studies):
  Study 1 — MCII participants showed **significantly less anger-related negative affect after an
  anger induction** than controls.
- A registered adolescent RCT (NCT03693209, Sheffield) tests if-then plans against anger and
  aggression, with trigger-specific versus general-trigger arms.
- **Mechanism:** the cue automatically speeds recognition of and preparation for the response,
  measurable in milliseconds, **and it operates without the person consciously thinking about the
  plan.** That is a pause that does not require presence of mind in the moment — which matters,
  because presence of mind is the thing anger removes.
- One secondary source states the evidence is "robust to publication-bias correction and
  surviving the post-2015 replication crisis." **Verify before repeating.**

## 3. As a clinical practice: urge surfing. `RCT evidence, addiction populations`
Observing and tolerating an urge without acting on it:
- Urge surfing in college students → **decreased smoking over 7 days** versus a no-instruction
  control.
- Mindful acceptance reduced momentary craving to smoking cues through a **"bottom-up" mechanism
  of reduced reactivity** — not suppression.
- Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention vs standard RP vs treatment-as-usual RCT; *Craving to
  Quit* smartphone RCT, where more modules completed → greater reduction in the
  **craving→smoking association**.

That last phrasing is the useful one: the practice weakens the **link** between the internal
state and the behaviour, rather than removing the state.

## Why this matters more than the SEL evidence

**It is the same architecture, already formalised and already tested.** "If I feel anger rising,
then I will [pause / breathe / state the boundary]" *is* an implementation intention.
Darryl's emotion → where it points → how you execute it is the if-then structure with the
emotion as the cue. And implementation intentions carry **d=0.65 across 642 tests** — larger and
better replicated than anything in the SEL corpus this register has catalogued.

**It also survives the anger problem.** [[I4]] rules out voicing anger aggressively; [[T3-Q2]]
rules out naming-then-reframing; [[P4c]] rules out venting. An if-then plan is none of those —
it substitutes a pre-selected response at the moment of the cue, before appraisal work begins.

## The SEL programs, for completeness
PATHS uses the **Turtle Technique** and Control Signals Poster; RULER uses the **Meta-Moment**.
Both put stop-and-breathe *before* the response. But: **PATHS meta-analysis overall ES = 0.11**
(SEL skills 0.16), below Cipriano's g=0.194; the Turtle Technique has one paywalled mechanisms
paper and an extended case study with **no effect size located**; and **no Meta-Moment
evaluation exists that I could find** — the returns were RULER's own teaching guides, school
blogs, and a Teachers Pay Teachers listing. **The component is unevaluated inside the programs
that teach it.** The evidence for the pause comes from elsewhere entirely.

**Retrieval, in order:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006; the 642-test 2024 meta-analysis (*European
Review of Social Psychology* 36(1)); the MCII-anger paper (*Frontiers in Psychology* 2018,
9:1838); results from NCT03693209 if posted.

### P5. Interventions move describing feelings and externally-oriented thinking, but not identifying feelings. `CONTESTED — the most important open question in the lesson`
**For:** Mazza 2026, meta-analysis, 47 studies, controlled: DIF g=−0.06 ns, DDF −0.39,
EOT −0.45. Identifying is the facet carrying nearly every outcome in Tier 1.
**Against:** Zompa 2025, 170 adults with binge eating disorder, 16 weeks of DBT modules
that explicitly teach identifying and labelling: DIF d=−0.27 p<.01, while DDF (−0.13) and
EOT (−0.22) did **not** move — the exact reverse.
**Hypothesis worth testing:** interventions that explicitly teach identifying move
identifying; the pooled average of interventions that mostly don't teach it doesn't.
**Blocker:** Zompa has no control group and is one clinical population. No controlled trial
of this appears to exist.

### P6. Emotional awareness measured by LEAS can be trained. `WEAKENED — three of the six citations identified, and they are thin`
Lane 2021 asserts psychoeducation and psychotherapy improve LEAS, citing six studies
(Burger 2016; Montag 2014; Neumann 2017; Radice-Neumann 2009; Subic-Wrana 2005; Killgore
2020) and **reports no effect size or sample size for any of them.**

**Maroti 2018's Table 1 and reference list identify three of the six, and none is a
controlled training trial:**
- **Burger et al. 2016** — "The effects of a novel psychological attribution and emotional
  awareness and expression therapy for chronic musculoskeletal pain: **a preliminary,
  uncontrolled trial**." n=72, mean age 49, 79% female. *Uncontrolled* by its own title.
- **Neumann, Malec & Hammond 2017** — "Reductions in alexithymia and emotion dysregulation
  after training emotional self-awareness following traumatic brain injury: **a phase I
  trial**." **n=24.**
- **Subic-Wrana et al. 2005** — **I was wrong to dismiss this one, and it is the largest of the
  three.** I recorded it as "a cross-sectional measurement study, not a training study" because
  its title is "a comparison of two different measures of alexithymia." It is **pre-post**:
  **N = 394 at onset, N = 249 at the end of multimodal psychodynamic treatment.** And the
  reported result is directly on point — **the LEAS captured change in emotional awareness with
  treatment while the TAS-20 captured change in negative affect instead.** (`secondary source`,
  primary text still not read.) Maroti separately used its baseline data as cross-sectional
  correlation (n=386), which is what misled me.
  **What it does and does not show:** uncontrolled pre-post in a psychosomatic ward with a
  multimodal treatment — so it does **not** establish that awareness training works, or which
  component moved anything. It does show LEAS moves with treatment and moves **distinctly from
  distress**, which is the discriminant-validity result [[M3]] and [[M4]] both need.

**Revised expectation:** the trainability claim rests, at least in part, on one uncontrolled
trial, one phase I trial of 24 people, and one study that is not about training. Montag 2014,
Radice-Neumann 2009 and Killgore 2020 are still unexamined and could change this — **Killgore
2020 remains the highest-value single retrieval**, since it is the most recent and the only one
not yet shown to be small or misattributed.
**Why this is the highest-value retrieval in the project:** LEAS is performance-based and
uncorrelated with negative affect. If awareness trains on LEAS, M2's ambiguity resolves
toward "real effect," and P5's contradiction becomes answerable on an uncontaminated
measure.

### P8. Emotion-understanding training in children works — at roughly half the headline size once publication bias is corrected, and worst in classrooms. `SUPPORTED`
**Sprung, Münch, Harris, Ebesutani & Hofmann, 2015**, "Children's emotion understanding:
A meta-analysis of training studies," *Developmental Review* 37:41–65 (**full text read**).
PRISMA. 17 articles yielding 19 studies/experiments, seven countries, ages 35–207 months
(mean 86 months ≈ 7 years, median 103). The abstract states 749 children; the study
characteristics section states 1,308 — **discrepancy in the paper itself, do not print
either figure without checking.**

**The headline figures are within-group pre-post, not controlled.** Both are reported:

| Level (components) | Within-group g | Controlled g | Studies with a control |
|---|---|---|---|
| **External** (recognition, external cause, reminder) | 0.62 [0.47, 0.78] | 0.60 [0.41, 0.78] | 10 of 25 comparisons |
| Mental (desire, belief, hiding) | 0.31 [0.11, 0.50] | 0.55 [0.32, 0.78] | 5 studies |
| Reflective (regulation, mixed, morality) | 0.64 [0.50, 0.79] | 0.68 [0.45, 0.91] | 7 studies |

**Publication bias is the finding that matters, and it hits the recognition level hardest.**
Trim-and-fill imputed 10 missing studies for the external level, dropping it from
**g=0.62 to g=0.34 [0.17, 0.51]** — nearly halved. Mental: 0.31 → 0.22. Reflective:
0.64 → 0.52. Fail-safe N for the mental level exceeded its critical value by only three
studies, and the authors say to interpret it with caution.

**So the defensible figure for training the recognition-and-labelling level is g≈0.34,
small-to-medium.**

**Four moderator findings that bear on this lesson:**
1. **The classroom was the weakest setting** for external aspects — classroom g=0.37,
   lab 0.66, a separate area in school 0.89. Group settings beat individual for external
   (0.72 vs 0.36); individual beat group for reflective (0.88 vs 0.42).
2. **Focused training beat comprehensive training.** Programs targeting external aspects
   only reached g=0.83 on external outcomes; programs combining all three levels reached
   0.36. The authors suggest adding complexity may confuse children still learning the
   basics.
3. **Dose was null again — the fifth independent null in this register.** Number of
   sessions: ns at all three levels. Length of the training period: ns at all three. Only
   *length of each session* moderated reflective outcomes (β=0.19, p<.05).
4. **Reflective gains faded with time** — effects decreased with longer post-test delay
   (β=−0.19, p<.01). External and mental did not.

Also: **study quality did not moderate** any effect; typically developing and clinical
samples did not differ; European training produced larger external-aspect effects than
North American (0.96 vs 0.41).

**Two limitations the authors state that matter for how far this generalises:** "very few
studies included a control group," and the search term was deliberately narrow — they
considered but rejected "recognition or identification" as search terms, so **this is not
the whole emotion-recognition training literature.**

### P9. SEL programs universally teach emotion recognition and almost never teach emotional blends. `VALIDATED — content mapping, not outcomes`
**O'Grady & Nag, 2024**, "Promoting emotion understanding in middle childhood: A systematic
review of school-based SEL programs," *Social and Emotional Learning: Research, Practice,
and Policy* 4:100068 (**full text read**, open access CC-BY). 38 programs across 54 studies
in 20 countries, Grades 3–5, studies published 2010–2020, searched December 2020. PRISMA;
11,225 records screened; studies rated 1–4 for quality with 1s and 2s excluded.

**This is a content-mapping review. It codes what programs teach, not what they achieve.**
It reports no outcome effect sizes at all, and must never be cited as evidence that SEL
works.

Programs mapped against a 10-component framework (nine from Pons et al. 2004, plus
'decision/action' from Crick & Dodge 1994):

| Component | Programs (of 38) |
|---|---|
| **Recognition** — identify and label emotions via faces and emotion terms | **38** |
| Regulation | 33 |
| Decision/action | 33 |
| External cause | 30 |
| Belief | 26 |
| Desire | 20 |
| Morality | 12 |
| **Mixed** — feeling opposing emotions simultaneously | **4** |
| Reminder — present cues triggering past emotion | 4 |

**Three findings that bear directly on this lesson:**
1. **Every single program teaches recognition.** Naming emotions is the universal floor of

2. **Only 4 of 38 teach blends, and 1 of 38 teaches that expression can mask inner state.**

### P11. What Durlak et al. 2011 actually found about SAFE and about who delivers. `VALIDATED — full text read`
**Durlak, Weissberg, Dymnicki, Taylor & Schellinger, 2011**, *Child Development*
82(1):405–432 (**full text read**). 213 universal programs, 270,034 students, studies
1955–2007, 47% randomized, 87% US, 83% rated SAFE. Grand mean **g=0.30 [0.26, 0.33]**,
I²=91%. Outcomes at post: skills 0.57, attitudes 0.23, positive social behaviour 0.24,
conduct problems 0.22, emotional distress 0.24, academic 0.27 (an 11-percentile gain).
At follow-up (33 studies, mean 92 weeks): 0.11–0.32, all still significant.

**SAFE does not gate whether a program works — it gates whether skills are acquired.**

| Outcome | Met SAFE | Did not meet SAFE |
|---|---|---|
| **Skills** | **0.69** | **0.01** (CI −0.57 to 0.60), N=5 |
| **Positive social behaviour** | **0.28** | **0.02** (CI −0.21 to 0.26), N=13 |
| Attitudes | 0.24 | **0.16, still significant** |
| Conduct problems | 0.24 | **0.16, still significant** |
| Academic performance | 0.28 | **0.26, still significant** |
| Emotional distress | 0.28 | 0.18, ns |

Non-SAFE programs produced significant effects in three of six domains. So "programs
produce meaningful gains only when SAFE" is too strong; the precise finding is that SAFE
separates programs where children actually *acquire the skills* from programs that shift
attitudes, conduct and grades without demonstrable skill gain.
- **Caveats the paper carries:** 176 of 213 programs were rated SAFE, so the comparison
  cells are tiny (skills N=5). SAFE was coded **dichotomously** because reports rarely
  said how much of each practice was used, and the authors state it would be preferable to
  code it continuously. And the SAFE skills figure is the **one place trim-and-fill moved
  anything materially: 0.69 → 0.45.**

**Delivery: teachers were not superior to outside specialists.** Table 2:
- **Class by teacher**: significant on all six outcomes (skills 0.62, academic 0.34).
- **Class by non-school personnel**: significant on three (skills **0.87** — *higher* than
  teacher-led — attitudes 0.14, conduct 0.17, and that last one lost significance after
  correcting for nested designs). Social behaviour, emotional distress and academic
  performance were all non-significant.
- The paper's claim is that teachers *can* deliver these programs successfully and outside
  personnel are not required — Hypothesis 2 was about teacher-led producing significant
  outcomes, **not about beating specialists.** Academic performance improved only under
  school personnel. Durlak **did not code teacher training at all**; that variable comes
  from Shi & Cheung 2024.

**Two findings that cut against "more and bigger is better":**
1. **Multi-component programs were not superior** — the authors' hypothesis failed. Multi-
   component effects on skills (0.12, CI −0.35 to 0.60) and social behaviour (0.19) were
   non-significant where teacher-led were significant. Explanation offered: multi-component
   programs were *less* likely to be SAFE (65% vs 90%) and *more* likely to report
   implementation problems (31% vs 22%).
2. **Program duration was negatively related to skill gains (r=−.25),** as was students'
   mean age (r=−.27). **Longer programs produced smaller skill effects.** Mean 40.8
   sessions, median 24; 77% of programs ran under a year. This is the sixth dose finding in
   this register and the first that is negative rather than null.

**Implementation mattered as much as design:** programs with reported implementation
problems were significant on only two outcomes; programs with monitored, problem-free
implementation were significant on all six (skills 0.86). But **43% of studies did not
monitor implementation at all** and were excluded from that analysis.

### P14. A methodology for isolating active components exists and 208 studies have used it, but it has not been assembled into an answer. `SUPPORTED — fetch summary, primary text not read`

behaviour, conduct problems and distress; 11 for academics; **23 for SEL skills.**

**After-school is where SAFE matters absolutely.** Non-SAFE after-school programs produced
**nothing** on any outcome — attitudes 0.04, social behaviour 0.00, conduct −0.03, academics
≈0 — while SAFE ones reached 0.32, 0.41, 0.35 and 0.17. Universal non-SAFE programs at least
moved attitudes, conduct and grades; after-school non-SAFE moved nothing.

### P12. The universal-versus-targeted comparison does not come from Durlak or Cipriano. `OPEN — source misattributed`
The figures circulating as universal g≈0.35 versus targeted g≈0.48 (skills .44, problem
behaviours .50, from 15 studies) **cannot come from either headline meta-analysis, because
both exclude targeted programs by design.** Durlak states it explicitly: "we were
interested in interventions for the entire student body (universal interventions) and thus
did not examine programs for indicated populations, that is, for students already
demonstrating adjustment problems. These latter programs have been evaluated in a separate
report (**Payton et al., 2008**)." Cipriano 2023 is likewise titled and scoped to universal
school-based SEL.

**Payton, Weissberg, Durlak, Dymnicki, Taylor, Schellinger & Pachan, 2008**, *The positive
impact of social and emotional learning for kindergarten to eighth-grade students: Findings
from three scientific reviews*, CASEL — **a technical report from the advocacy organisation,
not a peer-reviewed article.** That is the likely source and it needs retrieving before any
targeted-versus-universal figure is used.

**Also unsupportable as stated:** "the two largest meta-analyses in educational psychology."
The arithmetic checks (637 studies, 845,395 students), but educational psychology contains
far larger syntheses, and the two are not independent — Cipriano 2023 extends the same
research tradition with overlapping authorship lineage.

### P10. The headline SEL meta-analyses are located but not usable yet, and the comparator is the whole question. `OPEN`
- **Durlak, Dymnicki, Taylor, Weissberg & Schellinger, 2011**, *Child Development*
  82(1):405–432. 213 programs, N=270,034, grand mean **d=0.30 [0.26, 0.33]**, six outcome
  categories ranging 0.22–0.57 (secondary-source figures). **A fetch-and-summarize pass on
  this PDF produced confident, structured claims with no numbers attached and asserted that
  emotion identification was "measured separately" — do not trust it. The PDF is downloaded
  and unread.**
- **Cipriano, Strambler, Naples, Ha, Kirk, Wood et al., 2023**, *Child Development*
  94(5):1181–1204. 424 studies, 252 programs, 53 countries, **N=575,361**. The current
  state of the art. Effect sizes not yet obtained.
- **Taylor, Oberle, Durlak & Weissberg, 2017**, *Child Development* 88(4):1156–1171 —
  follow-up effects specifically.

**The comparator question, which decides how much any of this is worth.** The MYRIAD trial
(Kuyken and colleagues, 2022) delivered 10 mindfulness lessons to **8,376 students across
~85 UK schools** and found **null effects on all primary outcomes** — depression risk,
social-emotional-behavioural functioning, wellbeing — against **teaching as usual, in a
system where SEL provision was already widespread** (SEAL had reached 90% of UK primary
and 70% of secondary schools by 2010). It also reported possible **worsening** for students
already at risk of mental health problems. Home practice was near-absent: mean 1.16 on a
0–5 frequency scale, falling to 0.83 at follow-up.

**So: SEL meta-analyses pooling mostly no-treatment and standard-curriculum controls report
d≈0.30; the largest trial run against a genuinely active baseline returned nulls and
possible harm.** This is the same waitlist-versus-active-control gap that appears in the DBT
meta-analysis. Every SEL figure entering this register must record its comparator, and no
SEL claim reaches `SUPPORTED` on no-treatment-controlled evidence alone.

### P7. Body mapping has no trial as a standalone practice, but is a component of one placebo-controlled trial (Smith 2024). `SEARCHED and CORRECTED 14 Aug 2026`
**Downgraded 13 Aug 2026 from `VALIDATED (as an absence)`.** This was reached by not finding
body-mapping trials in what we happened to read — the same route that produced two false
absence claims the same day. **No deliberate search has been run.** Until one is, this claim
says nothing.

What is actually verified: Nummenmaa 2014 maps where 701 people report feeling emotions and is
**descriptive**. That part stands.

**Related, and it revises what I recorded about interoception:** the 2025 meta-analysis
(mindfulness → **self-reported** interoception, g=0.31, 29 RCTs, N=2,191) is not the only
evidence. A search on 13 Aug found a **5-day biofeedback-guided interoceptive training that
improved objective interoceptive accuracy — heartbeat detection — against controls**, plus MAIA
gains on "Awareness of Negative Emotional States" and "Not Distracting" (2025 bioRxiv preprint,
**not peer-reviewed, primary text not read**). And a 3-day mindfulness RCT (N=40) raised
interoceptive sensibility against an **active** control. So the Garfinkel objection — that
training moves self-report while leaving objective accuracy untouched — is weaker than this
register previously implied. **Retrieval priority: get the biofeedback trial's primary text.**

## Tier 3 — Q3: does awareness of a specific emotion help manage that emotion?

**This is the lesson's real question and it has four studies.** Two point opposite ways.

### Q1. Naming fear out loud *during* real exposure improved outcomes a week later. `SUPPORTED, UNREPLICATED`
Kircanski 2012, RCT, N=88 spider-fearful adults, four arms. One week later, different
spider, different room: skin conductance d=0.85 vs reappraisal, 0.74 vs distraction, 0.64
vs exposure alone. Dose-response: more fear words, more reduction.
**Limits:** no self-reported fear benefit at any timepoint; approach behaviour only
marginal; one emotion, one paradigm, one study.

### Q2. Naming a feeling *before* reappraising made the reappraisal worse. `SUPPORTED`
Ariely 2026, two pre-registered experiments (N=111, N=115), replicating Nook 2021.
Interaction ηp²=.14 and .07. Effects washed out by 1–2 day follow-up.
**Consequence:** the order matters, and awareness is not a free precursor to other methods.

### Q3. Low differentiation does not change which regulation strategies people select — it makes the strategies work less well. `SUPPORTED — abstract read verbatim 18 Aug 2026`
**Kalokerinos EK, Erbas Y, Ceulemans E & Kuppens P (2019)**, "Differentiate to Regulate: Low
Negative Emotion Differentiation Is Associated With Ineffective Use but Not Selection of
Emotion-Regulation Strategies," *Psychological Science* 30(6):863–879, PMID 30990768.
Two experience-sampling studies, **N = 200 (34,660 measurements) and N = 101 (6,282
measurements)**.

Verbatim: "we found **few relationships between differentiation and the selection** of
putatively adaptive or maladaptive strategies. Instead, we found interactions between
differentiation and strategies in predicting negative emotion. **Among low differentiators,
all strategies (Study 1) and four of six strategies (Study 2) were more strongly associated
with increased negative emotion than they were among high differentiators.**"

Authors' own conclusion: "effective regulation may underlie differentiation benefits."

**CORRECTED 18 Aug.** This entry previously said low differentiators "used reappraisal and
social sharing **more**, not less," and called it a "scattergun" approach. **Neither is in the
abstract, and the paper's title says the opposite: selection was largely unrelated to
differentiation.** The finding is about effectiveness, not choice. The wrong version was
repeated twice in conversation before the paper was retrieved.

**Why it matters:** the authors state the lesson's own thesis — the benefit of telling
feelings apart runs through what you then do, not through the telling itself.

### Q4. Finer anger distinctions go with less aggression on angry days. `SUPPORTED, CORRELATIONAL ONLY`
Pond 2012, three daily-diary studies, N=628 undergraduates: 17%, 43% and 16% less
aggression. Authors state the findings "may not reveal causal relationships"; heavy
reliance on single-item daily measures.

## Retired — checked and not to be re-adopted

- **People out of touch with their own feelings become unusually perceptive about others.**
  False; see O3.
- **Naming a feeling makes you feel better in the moment.** Not supported where it matters:
  no self-report benefit facing a live spider (Kircanski 2012) or real public-speaking
  anxiety (Niles 2015, where heavier labellers reported *worse* improvement). It does move
  self-report when viewing unpleasant images in a lab (Burklund 2014).
- **Seah & Coifman 2022 supports a self-injury claim.** It doesn't; the one self-injury
  study inside it was not independently significant. Use Norman 2020.
- **Kashdan 2010's "~40% less alcohol."** Students are the population where the substance
  link is weakest. Use Honkalampi 2022.
- **Alexithymia causes physical symptoms via misreading arousal.** The test failed: people
  with irritable bowel syndrome did not exceed people with inflammatory bowel disease
  (Ismaiel 2024), which is the comparison that would show it.
- **Alexithymia and blood pressure.** OR 3.15 but CI [1.14, 8.74], k=5, I²=87.5%, and
  prevalence declining by publication year. Too fragile to use.

## Known gaps — literatures this project has NOT reviewed

Listed so that absence of a claim is not mistaken for absence of evidence. This register
was built from what happened to get read, not from a systematic survey, and the first
version wrongly turned "we didn't look" into "there's nothing there."

1. **Positive emotional granularity as its own subject** — Tugade & Fredrickson 2004 and
   the broaden-and-build line. Not opened. Directly caused the O1 error.
2. **Ozomaro and colleagues, 2025**, "Emotional granularity in health and psychopathology:
   A scoping review," *Journal of Psychiatric Research* 190:277–295. Paywalled, abstract
   only. Its framing emphasises **social and functional** outcomes and neurobiology, and
   describes lower granularity across mood, anxiety, substance use, schizophrenia, PTSD,
   personality and eating disorders. This is the broadest map of the field that exists and
   we are working without it.
3. **Emotion regulation strategies on a common metric** — Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012, plus
   the Augustine & Hemenover 2013 critique. Would place labelling against reappraisal.
4. **Emotional clarity (Trait Meta-Mood Scale)** — a fifth construct. One located finding:
   emotional clarity and granularity were **uncorrelated** (Boden 2013).
5. **Interoception's own outcome literature** beyond the anxiety review and the mindfulness
   meta-analysis.
6. **Empathy specifically** — Vanheule et al. 2010, "Alexithymia in the interpersonal
   domain: A general deficit of empathy?" and the parent-youth dyad work finding actor
   effects on **cognitive** but not **affective** empathy.
7. **Emotion differentiation vs LEAS head-to-head** — Lane 2021 states "few direct tests"
   exist. The two literatures we treat as one have barely been compared.
