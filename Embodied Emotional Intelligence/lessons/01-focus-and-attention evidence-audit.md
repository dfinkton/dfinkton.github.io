# Evidence section audit — Lesson 1 (Focus and Attention)

Working through each paragraph in "The Evidence" against three questions:
1. **Value** — does this paragraph tell the reader something they can use, or is it filler/decoration?
2. **Jargon/numbers** — is every technical term or raw statistic load-bearing (referenced again, changes interpretation), or is it clutter a general reader can't use?
3. **Accuracy/framing** — does the supporting detail match the bold headline claim, and is anything stated more strongly than the citation supports?

Not applied to the lesson yet — this is a review document only.

## Does It Work?

**Goyal 2014 (paragraph 1)** — already fixed in the live file (screening-funnel cut, raw d-values cut, "scale you already know" replaced with a real comparison). Not revisited here.

**Kabat-Zinn 3-year follow-up (paragraph 2)** — **No changes.** This is the model for how the rest of the subsection should read: states the finding plainly (18 of 22 reached, still significant), immediately gives the real methodological limitation (no comparison group — can't rule out that a caring weekly meeting, not the technique, did the work), and explicitly hands off to "the effect-size evidence above... comes from the studies that followed" so the reader knows why the next paragraphs exist. No unexplained jargon, no decorative numbers.

**Hofmann 2010 / diagnosed populations (paragraph 3)** — **Needs the most work in this subsection.** Four studies stacked in one paragraph (Hofmann, Hoge, Kuyken, Goldberg), carrying six raw numbers: g=0.63, g=0.59, g=0.97, g=0.95, plus two percentages, plus "38% versus 49%."
- The g-values are the same problem as Goyal's original d-values — meaningless to a reader without stats background, and the paragraph already does the real communicative work in words: "these are simple before-and-after comparisons... so they naturally run higher" and "the effect nearly doubles." That sentence explains *why* the numbers are bigger and *by how much*, in plain terms. The four g-values sitting inside it are decoration on a point already made.
- The percentages are a different category — "two-thirds of patients... responded, against 40% in the comparison group" and "38% versus 49%" are intuitive without any stats background. I'd keep those.
- One caveat sentence in here is doing real, necessary work and should stay as-is: "Unlike Goyal's active-controlled numbers above, these are simple before-and-after comparisons within the same patients, so they naturally run higher." Without it, a reader who just saw Goyal's (now qualitative) numbers would have no way to know these are a weaker design, not a bigger effect.
- Suggested cut: drop "g=0.63 for anxiety, g=0.59 for depression" and "g=0.97 for anxiety, g=0.95 for depression," keep "nearly doubles" and the two percentage comparisons.

**Hilton 2017 / pain (paragraph 4)** — Two bare numbers with no anchor at all: "SMD=0.32" and "SMD=0.15." Unlike Goyal, there's no comparison given to make these mean anything — a reader can't even guess whether 0.32 is big or small. The one detail worth keeping is the reliability point: "zero heterogeneity across the 12 trials that measured it" is a real, honest signal (this is an unusually consistent finding, worth more trust than most numbers in this section) — but "heterogeneity" is jargon. Suggest: cut both SMD values, translate "zero heterogeneity" to something like "found consistently across all 12 trials that measured it."

**Dunning 2019 / kids and teens (paragraph 5)** — **This is a real problem, not a style preference.** The paragraph's entire point is a magnitude comparison: "The depression number alone is larger than the adult general-population effect from Goyal's review above (d=0.23–0.30)." But Goyal's paragraph no longer states d=0.23–0.30 anywhere — we cut it. This sentence now points the reader back to a number that isn't there. Two ways to fix it: restore some minimal numeric anchor to Goyal's paragraph, or reword this sentence to make the comparison without requiring the reader to check a number against another number ("larger than the adult effect described above" with no digits at all). Separately, "mindfulness itself d=0.42" is unclear on its own — it's measuring how much trait mindfulness increased, not a symptom outcome, and reads as a stray fragment without a one-clause gloss. "Anxiety and stress d=0.18" is the least necessary number in the paragraph — could be cut without losing anything since the headline claim is really about depression.

**Ye 2024 / adults over 60 (paragraph 6)** — "g=0.53 depression, g=0.43 anxiety" is redundant with "moderate effects for both" three words earlier — the paragraph already characterizes the size in words, then repeats it in a unit the reader can't parse. Suggest cutting the g-values entirely; "moderate effects for both — a smaller evidence base than the general adult literature, but the same direction" already says everything a reader needs.

**Gliske 2026 / pregnancy (paragraph 7)** — "SMD 0.71 and 0.73" is decoration on top of "landing on nearly identical numbers," which already communicates the real point (two independent reviews agreed closely). Suggest cutting the parenthetical. By contrast, "Anxiety improved in every review that measured it (9 of 9), depression in all but one (10 of 11)" should stay — count ratios like this are intuitive without any stats background, unlike standardized effect sizes, and they're doing real work showing consistency across independent reviews. The closing sentence on stress (floor effects in already-healthy samples) is already plain-language and needs no change.

## What Drifting Costs

This subsection is already in noticeably better shape than "Does It Work?" — most paragraphs use plain percentages or no numbers at all, and several are already the kind of model the numbers-heavy paragraphs above should be edited toward.

**LaFreniere 2020 / chronic worry (paragraph 1)** — **No changes.** "91.4% did not come true" and "the most common individual result was 100%" are plain percentages, immediately readable without stats background. The closing caveat — these were people selected for worrying far more than most, so this measures the worry that brings people to treatment, not ordinary worry — stops the finding from overgeneralizing. This is a good model paragraph.

**Nolen-Hoeksema 2000 / rumination (paragraph 2)** — **No changes.** No numbers at all, one plain sentence, correlational framing stated honestly ("predicts," not "causes"). Appropriately brief for a widely-replicated finding — doesn't need sample size or methodology spelled out to be trustworthy here, unlike some of the newer or more surprising claims elsewhere in the section.

**Killingsworth & Gilbert 2010 / mind-wandering (paragraph 3)** — **No changes.** "46.9%" is a plain percentage matching the headline's "about half the time." The explanation of why wandering-before-mood-dip (rather than after) is causally suggestive is done in plain English without needing to name "time-lagged analysis" — this is exactly the move the jargon-heavy paragraphs above should be making.

**Dittmar 2014 / materialism (paragraph 4)** — **No changes, and worth calling out as a model.** No effect-size number at all — the paragraph conveys magnitude with "modest," names where the association is strongest in plain terms, and states the correlational limitation directly. Proof that the honest, calibrated version of a finding doesn't require a stats-literate reader.

**Watkins 2008 / concrete vs. abstract thinking (paragraph 5)** — **No changes.** No numbers, vivid concrete contrast ("why am I like this?" vs. step-by-step), plain closing line. Another model paragraph.

**Takeaway for this subsection:** nothing to fix. Worth noting in the final report that the jargon-number problem isn't uniform across the Evidence section — it's concentrated in specific subsections (so far, "Does It Work?"), which suggests those got written or edited at a different time than this one.

## How the Practice Works

This is the longest subsection (11 paragraphs) and mostly strong — seven of the eleven need no changes at all. The two real issues are a missing jargon gloss and one badly overloaded paragraph.

**Anderson & Farb 2018 / anchor choice (paragraph 1)** — **No changes.** All plain percentages, directly matches the headline claim (no universal best anchor, poor self-prediction).

**Retrospective anchor comparison (paragraph 2, no bold header)** — **No changes.** A genuinely skeptical paragraph — states the weak sample size and the shaky measurement tool (a consumer EEG headband) plainly, so the reader isn't oversold on a thin finding. This is the right way to present a study you don't fully trust.

**Hasenkamp 2012 / four phases (paragraph 3)** — **One small gap.** "Default mode network" and "executive regions" both get an immediate plain-English gloss ("the system that runs when people are left alone with their thoughts"; "the ones that direct effort"). "Salience" does not — "the regions that flag salience" defines the term using the term. Suggest: "the regions that flag what's worth noticing" or similar.

**Slagter 2007 / attentional blink (paragraph 4)** — **No changes.** Describes the actual experimental task in fully concrete terms without ever naming "attentional blink." One of the best-written paragraphs in the section — worth using as the model when reworking others.

**Basso 2019 / small daily amounts (paragraph 5)** — **No changes.** Plain counts, honest attrition caveat (76 recruited, 42 finished), no jargon.

**Zainal & Newman 2023 / cued moments (paragraph 6)** — **Minor, optional.** "(d around 0.3 to 0.4)" sits right after "both modest effects," which already states the magnitude in words. The number is decorative here, not load-bearing — but it's one small parenthetical, not a pile of them, so this is a softer call than the Hofmann or Ye paragraphs above. The generalizability caveat (mostly white and female sample) is already plain-language and fine as is.

**Schumer 2018 / sustained practice (paragraph 7)** — **Minor, optional, different reasoning than Zainal.** "g=0.21" fading to "g=0.04... not reliably different from zero" is doing double duty — a magnitude claim ("small effect... near zero") and a demonstration of the actual publication-bias correction happening in front of the reader, which is a more sophisticated point than most paragraphs in this section make. There's a real argument for keeping the before/after numbers here specifically because the paragraph is showing its work, not just reporting a result. Flagging as optional rather than recommending the cut.

**The frequency-vs-duration paragraph (paragraph 8) — the one real structural problem.** Five separate studies (Fincham, Cearns, Bowles, Carmody, Parsons) stacked into a single paragraph with no break. Notably, this isn't a jargon problem — there is not one d, g, or SMD value anywhere in it; every number is a plain count or ratio ("more than twice as likely," "roughly two and a half times more," "about forty-five minutes a day, six days a week"). The problem is pure density: five citations, no structural pause, so the reader has to track which claim belongs to which study without any paragraph break to help. This is the same "reads like notes" complaint that started this whole audit, but caused by citation-stacking rather than unexplained statistics. Suggest splitting into two: one paragraph for "frequency beats duration," carrying Fincham, Cearns, and Bowles; a second for "but total practice still matters," carrying Carmody and Parsons — which is already how the content divides internally ("Total practice still matters —" is sitting mid-paragraph right now, doing the work of a paragraph break without being one).

**Cearns 2023 / short sessions hold up, then longer ones win, then short wins again (paragraph 9)** — **No changes.** Genuinely complex, three-phase finding (before 20th session / after 20th / after 80th), but the complexity is inherent to what was found, not a clarity problem — it's stated in concrete session-count milestones throughout, no jargon.

**Lim & Dinges 2010 / sleep vs. attention (paragraph 10)** — **No changes.** Plain description of all six measured domains, clear framing ("took the biggest hit... did not move at all"), nice concrete gloss on "simple attention."

**Sleep-first closing (paragraph 11, no bold header, no citation)** — **No changes.** Pure synthesis tying the finding back to the lesson's actual advice. No jargon, nothing to fix.

Three short paragraphs, no changes needed anywhere.

**Mrazek / Brandmeyer / 2021 review — mind wanders less (paragraph 2)** — **No changes.** Three citations, but each gets its own clean sentence and the whole paragraph is short — this is what the frequency-vs-duration paragraph in the previous subsection should look like after it's split. **Mrazek / working memory and reading comprehension (paragraph 3)** — **No changes.** Already glosses its one technical term inline — "working memory, meaning how much you can hold in mind and use at once" — exactly the pattern the Hasenkamp "salience" gap above should follow.

## Side Effects

Strong subsection overall — this is a safety-relevant topic and it's handled with real care. One jargon gap, otherwise clean.

**Britton 2021 / most effects are mild (paragraph 1)** — **No changes.** All plain percentages. The one piece of methodology mentioned — "a 44-item instrument put to them by someone who was not their teacher" — isn't decoration; it's a necessary detail that the Farias paragraph directly builds on two paragraphs later. Correctly included because it's load-bearing, unlike the screening-funnel numbers cut from "Does It Work?"

**Farias 2020 / why rates vary (paragraph 2)** — **No changes, and worth noting why this differs from the Goyal cut.** This paragraph's entire thesis *is* about methodology explaining the variance (3.7% in experimental studies vs. 33.2% in observational ones), so the screening number and study-type breakdown aren't incidental flourishes — they're the substance of the claim. The callback to Britton's 44-item interview also works correctly here (unlike the Dunning-references-cut-Goyal-numbers problem above) — this is a cross-reference that still has something to point back to.

**Psychotherapy comparison (paragraph 3, no header, no citation)** — **No changes, and worth calling out as the right way to do what the Goyal paragraph got wrong.** "Ordinary psychotherapy produces new or worsening symptoms in 42–51% of people... Meditation's lasting-effect rate sits inside that same range" gives the reader a real anchor without ever claiming they already had one — contrast with the original "put those on a scale you already know" line that got cut earlier in this session.

**Britton 2021 / rare experiences predict harm (paragraph 4)** — **One jargon gap.** "Executive dysfunction" is never glossed — everything else in this paragraph is either plain language or self-explanatory (insomnia, emotional blunting), but this one clinical term is dropped in unexplained. Suggest something like "executive dysfunction (trouble planning, organizing, or following through)." Everything else — the 6-to-14-times risk ratio, the percentage ranges — is plain and fine.

**Blunting/disturbed-self follow-up (paragraph 5, no header)** — **No changes.** Short, actionable, no jargon. Good closing safety note.

## Noticing and Allowing

**Lindsay 2018 / noticing alone didn't move the stress response (single paragraph)** — **No changes.** No jargon-numbers at all — the whole paragraph is conceptual description. Worth noting this one is unusually careful about what it does and doesn't claim ("acceptance was never tested on its own... one outcome, in one contrast... not the same as acceptance being what makes any practice work"). That level of hedging is earned here: this Evidence entry is the direct backing for a structural claim made early in the lesson's Overview ("Noticing and allowing work together... On its own, monitoring what you feel doesn't move the stress response"), so it needs to hold up carefully. Nothing to change.

## The Limits

**Britton / informal practice flag (paragraphs 1–2, one with header, one without)** — **No changes.** Plain counts (13.5 vs. 4.3 sessions a week), and the follow-up paragraph is a model of honest correlational hedging — it names the reverse-causation possibility directly ("people having a hard time may well practise informally more *because* they are having a hard time") rather than burying it. This is exactly the register the rest of the section should aim for when a finding is real but uncertain.

**Van Dam 2018 / the field's own critics (paragraph 3)** — **No changes.** Zero numbers, fully plain language, sharp closing line. Nothing to fix.

**Khoury 2013 / Grossman 2004 — comparison groups (paragraph 4) — flags the same cross-reference problem found in "Does It Work?", and this makes it a pattern, not a one-off.** This paragraph explicitly cites "the tightly controlled trials in Goyal and colleagues, 2014 (d=0.22–0.38)" as its comparison point — the exact range we cut from Goyal's own paragraph earlier this session. That's now **two separate paragraphs** (this one and the Dunning kids/teens paragraph in "Does It Work?") that depend on a number no longer stated anywhere in the section. This substantially strengthens the earlier recommendation: Goyal's paragraph needs at least the bare range "(d=0.22–0.38)" restored somewhere, even if the rest of the paragraph stays qualitative, because at least two other paragraphs point back to it by digit. Aside from that, the numbers in this paragraph itself (g=0.89, g=0.53–0.69, d=0.54) are structurally necessary, not decorative — the entire point is a magnitude comparison across differently-controlled designs, same reasoning as Dunning. Keep them.

**Foale 2024 / DeLuca 2018 — low-income and ethnoracial representation (paragraph 5)** — **No changes, borderline case worth noting.** "Screened 112 candidate studies... found only 12" is a screening-funnel number, the same category we cut from Goyal — but here it's arguably necessary rather than decorative, since the paragraph's whole point is how thin this specific evidence base is, and the size of the funnel (112 candidates down to 12 usable studies, 10 of them American) *is* the finding. Closer call than Farias above, but I'd leave it in. Closing line ("a narrow slice of the world") is honest and plain.

**ReSource Project setup (paragraph 1)** — **No changes, one very minor optional note.** "Metacognition and theory of mind" aren't formally defined, but the sentence structure illustrates both immediately after naming them ("seeing a thought as an event" for metacognition, "taking another person's view" for theory of mind) — the example does the gloss's job without a formal definition. Acceptable as is; could be made fully explicit but isn't a real gap the way "salience" or "executive dysfunction" were.

**Trautwein 2020 / findings (paragraph 2)** — **No changes.** No raw effect sizes anywhere. "Cortical thickness" is left unglossed but is close enough to self-explanatory in context (thickness of brain tissue) that it doesn't block understanding of the actual finding, which is about each person's brain change tracking their own specific gain.

## Summary and recommendations

**The pattern across the whole section:** the jargon-and-clutter problem is not uniform. It's concentrated almost entirely in **"Does It Work?"**, spottily present in one or two spots elsewhere, and absent everywhere else. Five of the eight subsections ("What Drifting Costs," "What the Road Looks Like," "Noticing and Allowing," most of "The Limits," "Not One Skill") needed no changes at all, and several individual paragraphs are strong enough to use as the model when rewriting the weak ones — particularly **Slagter 2007** (describes a real experimental task with zero jargon), **Dittmar 2014** (conveys magnitude in words alone, states the correlational limit directly), and the **psychotherapy comparison line** in Side Effects (gives the reader a real anchor without ever claiming they already had one — the fix the Goyal paragraph needed).

**Concrete changes recommended, in priority order:**

1. **Fix the two broken cross-references.** Cutting Goyal's raw d-values (d=0.22–0.38) broke two other paragraphs that cite that exact range: the Dunning kids/teens comparison in "Does It Work?" and the Khoury/Grossman comparison-groups paragraph in "The Limits." Restore at minimum the bare range "(d=0.22–0.38)" to Goyal's paragraph, or reword both dependent sentences to make the comparison without requiring a number to check against. This is the highest-priority item — it's not a style preference, it's a paragraph currently pointing at information that no longer exists.

2. **Trim the Hofmann 2010 paragraph** ("Does It Work?," paragraph 3) — cut the four raw g-values (0.63, 0.59, 0.97, 0.95), keep "nearly doubles" and the two percentage comparisons (two-thirds vs. 40%; 38% vs. 49%), keep the active-controlled-vs-before/after caveat sentence as is — it's necessary, not decorative.

3. **Cut two more bare, unanchored numbers:** Hilton 2017's "(SMD=0.32)" and "(SMD=0.15)" — translate "zero heterogeneity" to plain language instead ("found consistently across all 12 trials that measured it"); and Ye 2024's "(g=0.53 depression, g=0.43 anxiety)," which is redundant with "moderate effects for both" three words earlier.

4. **Split the frequency-vs-duration mega-paragraph** in "How the Practice Works" into two — Fincham/Cearns/Bowles ("frequency beats duration") and Carmody/Parsons ("but total practice still matters"). Not a jargon problem — every number in it is already a plain count or ratio — but five citations with no paragraph break is the same "reads like notes" problem from a different cause.

5. **Two small jargon glosses:** "salience" in the Hasenkamp paragraph and "executive dysfunction" in the Britton rare-experiences paragraph are the only two unexplained technical terms found in the entire section outside "Does It Work?" Both are one-clause fixes.

6. **Optional, lower priority:** Gliske 2026's "(SMD 0.71 and 0.73)" is decorative on top of "landing on nearly identical numbers" and could go. Zainal 2023's "(d around 0.3 to 0.4)" and Schumer 2018's g=0.21/g=0.04 are softer calls — both are doing at least some real work (Schumer's numbers demonstrate a publication-bias correction in action) and could reasonably be left as is.

**Not recommended for change:** the screening-funnel numbers in Farias 2020 and Foale 2024, even though they superficially resemble the Goyal cut — in both cases the funnel size is the substance of the paragraph's claim (methodology explains rate variance; the evidence base is this thin), not incidental methodological flavor the way it was in Goyal.
