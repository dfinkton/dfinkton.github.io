The Practice

When it comes to attention and focus, you are building a skill. It works much like building any other capacity.

Because this practice is thousands of years old, there are countless ways to do it, which can quickly become overwhelming. Different teachers and ancient texts will swear by their specific method. The reality is that you truly can develop your focus using almost anything. What you are training is a single event—noticing you have drifted, and coming back—and that event is the same no matter what you had been pointed at.

You can pick an anchor in your internal world. That might be the physical sensation of your breath, the tip of your nose, or a slow scan of your body. You might focus on the crown of your head, the points along your spine, your toes, or even your thoughts themselves.

But for some people, internal anchors just don't feel right. If focusing inward is uncomfortable or distracting, no big deal—start with something else. Give your attention to the external world. You can stare at the flame of a candle, a single spot on the wall, or the entire wall. You can watch a bug crawling on the sidewalk or a single blade of grass. Anything works. The "right" anchor is simply the one you are willing to use.

Try a few before you settle, because your guess about what will suit you is not very good. When researchers gave 82 people who had never meditated three anchors in a row—the breath, a repeated phrase, and a simple image on a screen—about half ended up preferring the breath and the rest split between the other two. More than half of them ended up preferring a different anchor than the one they had picked before trying (Anderson and Farb, 2018).

Personally, my favorite anchor to start a session with is a single external dot, or something equally small. I like looking at a tiny object because it gives me immediate visual feedback. The second my mind drifts, my eyes usually lose focus, and the object blurs. I instantly know my attention wandered. After about five or ten minutes of that, my mind is calm enough that I can easily switch to another anchor, whether that is something internal or a broader external scene.

You just have to find what works for you. There is no wrong way to do this, other than simply not paying attention.

And this is the most important part of the entire process: the moment you catch yourself not paying attention, you are already back. You haven't failed. Catching your mind wandering and pulling it back to your anchor is the practice. That is the repetition that builds the muscle. Each of those four moments—attention on the anchor, the mind leaving, the catch, the return—shows up as its own distinct activity in the brain (Hasenkamp and colleagues, 2012).

There is one exception to there being no wrong way, and it is worth naming. Sitting still with your own mind takes away the distraction that was covering whatever is underneath it. For most people what is underneath is boredom and a to-do list. For some—especially anyone carrying something that has never been dealt with—it is grief or fear arriving with nothing in the way. Watching the breath has its own version of this: for some people it makes breathing feel effortful and strange, and the strangeness turns into panic.

Neither of those means you are doing it wrong, and neither one means push harder. Make the sessions shorter. Keep your eyes open. Use an anchor outside your body—sound, a dot on the wall, your feet on the floor—instead of one inside it. And get another person involved rather than grinding through it alone. If the habit of leaving your body is old, deep, and familiar, Observation is the better place to start, and you can come back to this.

The key is to start small, keep track, and slowly increase the challenge. Again, this is no different than learning a sport or an instrument. If you try to sit still in silence for an hour on your very first day, it will likely be too difficult and you will stop.

Instead, try one minute a day for the first week. Just one minute, but do it every single day. The next week, go up to two minutes. Keep stepping it up week by week until you reach a level that works for your life. For myself, my sweet spot is about 20 to 40 minutes, twice a day, which is more than most people need. Yours might be different. The goal isn't to hit a specific number of minutes; it is to find the daily volume that keeps your mind anchored when you need it most.

Small daily amounts really do work, and they take a while. In one study, people who had never meditated were randomly assigned to either thirteen minutes a day of guided meditation or thirteen minutes a day of listening to a podcast, for eight weeks. Attention, memory and mood all improved in the meditation group—at eight weeks, but not yet at four (Basso and colleagues, 2019). If you are five weeks in and nothing has changed, that is roughly what the research would predict.

There is one way this is not like learning an instrument. With an instrument, effort is the main lever. With attention, sleep is. Across about seventy studies of short-term sleep loss, lapses of simple attention took the biggest hit of anything measured, while the ability to reason barely moved (Lim and Dinges, 2010). If you are sleeping five hours a night and practicing ten minutes a day, the ten minutes is not the lever. Fix the sleep first.

If you want a little more structure, there are several excellent apps and programs available, most of them free. Many of them include guided sessions where you practice focusing your attention on specific ideas—like compassion, forgiveness, impermanence, or gratitude.

If you are looking for guided support to start building that focus, here are a few good places to start:

Humin Wellbeing App: humin.org/wellbeing-tools/app — the Healthy Minds Program, built by the organization Richard Davidson founded. He is the senior author on the heat-pad study earlier in this lesson, which makes this the closest thing to practicing with the people doing the research. Free and ad-free; you do have to register.

Palouse Mindfulness: palousemindfulness.com — a complete eight-week Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction course, free, with no account, no signup, and no payment of any kind.

Insight Timer: insighttimer.com — the largest free library of guided practice anywhere. It does require an account, and most of the structured courses sit behind a paid tier.
