## The Challenge

Someone criticizes you, or an anxious thought surfaces, and your body reacts. Your jaw clenches. Your heart races. Your breathing quickens. Before you consciously choose how to respond, the feeling drives your behavior. You might snap at the person, walk out of the room, shut down, cry, throw something, or hit someone. You might go quiet in a way the other person is forced to interpret. You can run through this entire sequence without observing a single step, only noticing the damage after the fact.

When angry, people who struggle to identify exactly what they are feeling react more aggressively than people who understand their emotions clearly ([Pond and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22023359/)). The escalation is rarely a conscious choice. It happens because you never saw it coming.

In a relationship, your unchecked behavior triggers your partner. Your partner reacts to your outburst, and you react to their defense. Neither of you chose the pattern, but both of you keep it running. Couples in this position have the same argument repeatedly, and each repetition damages the relationship.

This dynamic dictates who stays in your life. If you cannot pinpoint what you feel, you cannot tell anyone what you need, forcing the people around you to guess. They will often guess incorrectly, and this leads to conflict. Over time you end up feeling alone even with them in the room, and it is that feeling that wears the relationship down ([Panahi and colleagues, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6259507/)).

Emotional blindness distorts how you perceive reality. When an emotion goes unrecognized, it frequently disguises itself as an objective fact. If you do not realize you are anxious, you simply believe a situation is dangerous. If you do not realize you are frustrated, you assume the project you are working on is impossible. Because you cannot see the emotional lens you are looking through, you are prone to making permanent decisions—quitting a job, ending a partnership, abandoning a goal—based on temporary internal states you do not even know you are experiencing.

When this difficulty reaches clinical levels, psychologists call it alexithymia: difficulty identifying or describing your own emotional states. When you cannot name what is happening, the only move left is to make it stop. The ways of making it stop fast are the ones that cost you later. People who struggle to identify their feelings harm themselves more ([Norman and colleagues, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sjop.12668)), think about suicide more ([Hemming and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599888/)), and drink and use more ([Honkalampi and colleagues, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sjop.12821)).

An emotion you never catch still runs in your body. Anger carried for years raises your risk of heart disease ([Chida and Steptoe, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19281923/)). In the two hours after an angry outburst, the risk of a heart attack runs more than twice as high ([Mostofsky and colleagues, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3753402/)). Some people feel emotional distress as physical illness instead, and the ones who report the most unexplained physical complaints are exactly the ones who have the hardest time telling an emotion from a sensation ([De Gucht and Heiser, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12726898/)).

The ultimate challenge of an unobserved emotion is that it operates quietly in the dark. You cannot change an emotional loop that you are unaware of. You cannot reconsider what an event means to you if the event never clearly came into focus. To choose a different response, you have to catch yourself in the loop at some point. When this cycle runs unchecked, you are not actually making choices—you are stuck reacting in whatever ways you have been programmed to respond. The psychologist Carl Jung said it plainly: "Until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate."
