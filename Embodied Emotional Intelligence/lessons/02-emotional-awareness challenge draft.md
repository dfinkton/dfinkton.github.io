## The Challenge

Show a three-year-old a box and tell her that only scared people go inside it. Then hand her photographs one at a time and ask which ones belong. She puts in the scared face. She also puts in the angry face, the sad face, and the disgusted one.

She isn't guessing, and she isn't confused. At three, *scared* is a real category for her, with a firm edge. The edge just sits in a different place than yours does, and almost everything unpleasant falls inside it. Give her a box for sad faces instead and the angry and frightened ones go in there too ([Widen and Russell, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2008.01.002)).

What she has is one line down the middle. Feelings that are pleasant on one side, feelings that are unpleasant on the other. That is where all of us start, and learning to tell feelings apart means slowly drawing more lines inside those two groups. Children do this in a consistent order over several years. Happy separates out first, usually before a child turns three. Angry and sad come next, then scared and surprised at around four. Disgusted comes last, and most five-year-olds still don't have it — they file disgust under anger. So do plenty of adults.

The skill does not simply keep improving from there. Children get better at telling their feelings apart until early adolescence, and then it goes backwards for a while. Teenagers are worse at it than younger children are, and the low point comes at around age fifteen. After that it recovers, and it keeps improving for a very long time. People in their seventies tell their own feelings apart more finely than people in their twenties do ([Nook and colleagues, 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0956797618773357); [Carstensen and colleagues, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.4.644)).

What stops early is the teaching. Preschools have feelings charts on the wall. Somewhere around second grade the chart comes down and nothing takes its place. However well you can do this now, you mostly picked it up by accident — from how the adults around you named things, or from the fact that they didn't.

So ask an adult what they are feeling, and you will usually get an answer right away. Bad. Fine. Stressed. Off. Tired. Those are honest answers. They are also very broad, and a lot of us are still working with something close to the three-year-old's single line down the middle: good feelings on one side and bad ones on the other.

That line causes trouble of its own, before we get anywhere near the question of which feeling you are actually having. As soon as you decide a feeling is bad, you stop treating it as information and start treating it as a problem. Something has gone wrong, and you want it to stop.

But a feeling is not a malfunction. Think again about the dashboard in your car. When the oil light comes on, the light is not the problem. The light is the report. You would not solve anything by pulling the bulb out, even though the dashboard would look much better afterwards. Feelings work the same way. Anger, dread, and grief are unpleasant to have, and none of them is a fault. Each one is telling you something about your situation. If your first move on feeling something unpleasant is to get rid of it, you are pulling out bulbs, and you will do it without ever finding out what the light was for.

The second problem is that "bad" is too broad to act on, because different feelings are asking for different things, and sometimes for opposite things.

Loneliness and exhaustion are a good example. Loneliness is the gap between the connection you have and the connection you need, and it asks you to go toward people. Exhaustion after too much contact with people asks the exact opposite: it wants you to go away and be quiet for a while. Both of them can feel like a heavy, flat, unpleasant weight at the end of a long day. If all you can tell is that you feel bad, you have no way to choose between them, and you will sometimes cancel on a friend when seeing that friend is the thing that would have helped, or force yourself out to a party when what you needed was an empty room.

Or take grief and unfair treatment. Grief tells you that something you cared about is gone. There is nothing to solve; the work is carrying it. Feeling wronged by somebody is almost the reverse. It says a line got crossed and something can still be said or done about it. Treat grief like a problem to fix and you will exhaust yourself looking for a solution that does not exist. Treat being wronged like something to sit with and endure, and the thing that crossed the line stays in place.

When you cannot tell which one arrived, none of those specific responses is available to you. What is still available is making the feeling quieter, so that is what most people reach for. And the ways of making a feeling quieter quickly tend to be the ones that cost you later: a drink, a raised voice, another hour of scrolling, skipping the appointment, snapping at whoever happens to be nearby.

Researchers have measured what that costs, across a lot of different areas of life, and some of those findings are much better established than others.

The clearest one is anxiety, which surprises most people, because the popular version of this subject almost always leads with depression. The harder a time people have naming what they feel, the more anxiety they tend to carry, and of everything that has been studied here, anxiety is where the severity of the problem tracks most closely with the difficulty naming feelings ([Yeung and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/cps0000288); [Kashdan and Farmer, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4191833/)). Depression is the connection that has been checked the most times, by separate research teams working on separate continents, and they keep arriving at the same answer ([Liu, He and Hou, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1465286/full); [Li and colleagues, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25769520/)). Thinking about suicide is also strongly connected, and there the pattern has an odd shape worth knowing about: the link is much stronger to the thoughts than to the attempts ([Hemming and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599888/)). The largest differences of all turn up in eating disorders, where people with anorexia score further from people without it than almost any other group that has been compared ([Yeung and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/cps0000288)).

It shows up in the body as well as the mind. People who struggle to name their feelings sleep worse ([Alimoradi and colleagues, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34958823/)), get more headaches and migraines ([La Touche and colleagues, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8638568/)), and have more trouble with their stomachs and bowels ([Ismaiel and colleagues, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11474551/)). They also report more of the aches and pains that no medical test can account for, and those complaints are highest in exactly the people who have the hardest time telling an emotion apart from a physical sensation ([De Gucht and Heiser, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12726898/)).

The quick ways of quieting a feeling show up in the research too, in about the places you would expect. People who can make finer distinctions between their unpleasant feelings behave less aggressively on the days they are angriest ([Pond and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22023359/)). Drinking and drug problems are more common in people who cannot name what they feel ([Honkalampi and colleagues, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sjop.12821)), and so is deliberately hurting yourself ([Norman and colleagues, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sjop.12668)). Put binge eating, drinking, aggression and missed medical treatment together in one pile, and finer distinctions go with less of all of it ([Seah and Coifman, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000968)). The connection to self-harm is strongest in teenagers, which is worth pausing on, because that is also the age when the ability to tell feelings apart is at its lowest. The years when the skill is weakest are the years when not having it costs the most.

It reaches other people too. Adults who have trouble identifying and describing their feelings report being less satisfied with their relationships and their sex lives ([Humphreys, Wood and Parker, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2008.09.002)). Much of that seems to happen by way of loneliness: in one study of couples, once the researchers accounted for how lonely people felt, there was nothing left of the connection to explain ([Panahi and colleagues, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6259507/)).

There is a common belief that people who are out of touch with their own feelings become unusually good at reading everyone else, because they had to be. The research points the other way. People who tell their own unpleasant feelings apart more finely are also more accurate at reading other people's faces, and the two abilities rise and fall together rather than trading off ([Israelashvili and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30734635/); [Grynberg and colleagues, 2012](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0042429)). What is true is that some people watch others very closely. Close watching is not the same thing as accuracy, and knowing your own weather better makes you somewhat less wrong about other people's ([Erbas and colleagues, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550616633504)).

Almost all of that research watches people rather than changing anything, so for most of it we cannot say which came first, and it probably runs in both directions. Not being able to name what you feel makes hard days harder, and a long run of hard days will make it harder to tell your feelings apart ([Demiralp and colleagues, 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797612444903)). Twice, though, researchers have followed the same people forward over time to find out about the order, and both times the difficulty naming feelings came first. Low emotional awareness — meaning how well someone can tell what they are feeling — predicted mental health problems getting worse two years later ([Weissman and colleagues, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33758688/)). And in teenagers, difficulty naming feelings predicted self-harm five months later, rather than the other way around ([Norman and colleagues, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sjop.12668)).

None of this looks the same from one person to the next, and it is worth working out which version is yours, because they do not come apart in the same way.

For some people the problem really is the vocabulary. There is one word for the whole unpleasant end of the range, and it covers grief, resentment, dread, boredom and being hungry at four in the afternoon. For others the words are all there and the timing is wrong. They can tell you exactly what they felt and why, at eleven that night, hours after the conversation where knowing it would have made a difference.

Some people feel it clearly in the body and cannot get a word onto it: the chest goes tight, the jaw sets, the stomach drops, and none of it has a name. Other people have the reverse problem, where the word arrives without any physical sensation at all. They work out that they must be anxious because they have checked their email nine times in ten minutes, reasoning backwards from their own behavior while the body stays silent.

Then there are people with only two settings, fine and overwhelmed, and nothing registering on the way up. The first report arrives at an eight out of ten, which leaves no room to do anything early. And some people run everything through one channel, so that whatever happens comes out as anger, or comes out as sadness, or comes out as anxiety. Hurt is expressed as irritation. Fear is expressed as flat resignation. Which channel it is usually depends on which feeling was allowed where you grew up.

None of that is a character flaw, and most of it was learned somewhere it made sense. If naming a feeling out loud got you mocked, corrected or punished, then not naming it was the right call. If one emotion was permitted in your house and the rest were not, you became fluent in the one that was. If your safety depended on tracking somebody else's mood minute by minute, your attention went there, and there was not much left over for your own. And if what was happening inside you was more than a child could hold, then turning the volume down was not a mistake. It was the only thing available. Children who grow up around violence really do end up with less access to their own feelings, and that turns out to be part of how the harm carries forward into later life ([Weissman and colleagues, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33758688/)).

One more thing worth knowing is that this is not fixed. It moves. Stress on one day predicts a reduced ability to tell your feelings apart the next day, and the effect is specific to stress: anger, sadness, anxiety and loneliness do not do it ([Erbas and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000126)).

Which means the blunt version of this skill tends to show up on exactly the days you need the precise version. The argument, the deadline, the bad news. Those are the moments when everything collapses back into *bad*, and the only responses left are the ones that cost you.

The problem is not that you fail to notice something is wrong. You notice. The signal arrives in the body, usually before there are any words attached to it. What is missing is the next step: telling which signal it is, while it is still quiet enough to do something other than react.
