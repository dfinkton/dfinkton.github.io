## The Overview

Emotional awareness is the ability to notice what you are feeling. To make that awareness useful, you also have to put words to it. It is the shift from a vague "I feel bad" to a specific "I feel frustrated because I was interrupted" or "I feel anxious about tomorrow." This lesson teaches you how to build both skills through three core practices: a daily emotional check-in, a body scan, and a partner check-in.

The idea is simple: you already experience emotions, and those emotions drive your actions. The goal is to catch them as they happen and name them accurately. That skill—noticing and naming—is the foundation for everything else in this curriculum.

Emotional awareness is not emotional control. You are not trying to force yourself to be calm or change the feeling itself; you are just trying to see it clearly. When people cannot tell their feelings apart, they often use the wrong tools to cope. For example, they might try to handle exhaustion the exact same way they handle anger, which rarely works. But when you accurately identify what is happening inside, you can choose a response that fits the situation. With this clarity, people tend to act less aggressively, build better relationships, and cause less harm.

Two things will help you as you begin. First, your body feels emotion before your mind does. Anger shows up as heat and tension. Fear often feels like a drop in your stomach. If the first time you notice anger is when you hear yourself shouting, you have caught it too late. Second, your vocabulary limits your options. Most people stop at basic words like happy, sad, angry, and scared. But resentment requires a different response than anger, just as disappointment differs from sadness. More precise language gives you better choices for what to do next.

This work is hard. Paying close attention to your internal state can be uncomfortable, and stress makes it even harder to process those feelings. On the days you need this clarity the most, your words will be the hardest to find. That is why repetition matters. Building this skill takes months of consistent effort. By practicing these three techniques now when the stakes are low, the muscle memory will be there when the pressure hits.
