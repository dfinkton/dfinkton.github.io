## The Overview

Emotional awareness is the ability to notice what you're feeling and put words to it. Not just "I feel bad," but "I feel frustrated because I was interrupted" or "I feel anxious about tomorrow's meeting." This lesson teaches you how to build that ability through three practices: a daily emotional check-in, a body scan, and a partner check-in.

The takeaway is simple: you already feel things, and those feelings drive your actions. The question is whether you can catch them while they're happening and name them accurately. That skill — noticing and naming — is the foundation everything else in this curriculum builds on.

Emotional awareness is not the same as emotional control. You're not trying to change how you feel or make yourself calmer. You're trying to see what's there more clearly. People who do this tend to act less aggressively, have better relationships, and harm themselves less. But awareness alone doesn't fix anything — it just makes change possible. People who can't tell their feelings apart don't reach for worse strategies than anyone else. They reach for the exact same tools, but because they apply them blindly, those tools fail.

Three things to know before you start:

First, your body carries emotions before your mind catches up. Anger shows up as heat and tension. Fear shows up as a drop in your stomach. If the first time you notice anger is when you hear yourself shouting, you've detected it too late.

Second, you can't tell your feelings apart if you don't have words for them. Most people learn a handful of basic emotions — happy, sad, angry, scared — and stop there. But resentment is not the same as anger. Disappointment is not the same as sadness. The more precisely you can name what you feel, the more options you have for what to do about it.

Third, this is harder than it sounds. Most of us were never taught to pay attention to our internal states. We were taught to push through, stay busy, not make a fuss. Building this skill takes repetition, and it takes time. Plan on practicing consistently for a couple of months before you expect to see real change.

A few things to keep in mind. Awareness isn't always better — it depends on whether you have support. When support is low, being more aware can make things worse. Stress makes it harder to tell your feelings apart, so on the days you want these tools the most, you'll have the fewest words available. And knowing you're aware and actually being aware are two different things — people who say they're aware of their emotions don't necessarily show it when tested.
