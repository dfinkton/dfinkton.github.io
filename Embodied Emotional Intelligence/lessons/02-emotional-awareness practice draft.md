## The Practice

All three of these practices involve repetition. What you are building is the ability to catch a feeling while it is still happening and put a word to it. That skill comes from doing it many times when very little is at stake, so that the ability is there when something actually is. They work from different directions—on your own, through the body, and with another person. Start with the first, and add the others once it is running smoothly.

### The Daily Emotional Check-In

The emotional check-in takes about a minute or two and is simple enough to do standing in a hallway. You stop what you are doing, run down a fixed list of emotions, and note which ones are present and how strongly.

Set two to six alarms spread evenly across your morning, afternoon, and evening. Waiting until you spontaneously notice a feeling is the exact problem you are trying to solve—for the first few weeks, let the alarms do the noticing for you. When an alarm sounds, follow these steps:

**Rate the core emotions.** Run down this fixed list of eleven words and give each a score from 0 to 10 based on how strongly you feel it right now. Most will be 0, and you can pass over them in a second.

- **Curiosity:** The open, leaning-in feeling when something catches your interest and makes you want to look closer or understand it better.
- **Fear:** The tight, bracing sensation when you sense a threat, danger, or the possibility that something might go badly.
- **Joy:** A bright, lifting sensation when things are good. It can feel like a quiet internal warmth or a sudden burst of lightness and excitement.
- **Anger:** The hot, energized feeling that rises up when a boundary is crossed, when something is unfair, or when you are frustrated and blocked from what you want.
- **Peace:** The quiet, settled feeling when nothing is urgently pulling at your attention and you do not feel the need to change anything about the present moment.
- **Sadness:** The heavy, dropping sensation that comes with loss, disappointment, or recognizing that something you care about is gone.
- **Connection:** The secure, anchored feeling of being in tune with someone else, where you feel seen, understood, and part of a shared experience.
- **Guilt:** The heavy, uncomfortable feeling of remorse when you realize you have caused harm or violated your own standards. It often brings a sinking sensation, a dropped gaze, and the urge to hide or fix the mistake.
- **Caring:** The outward-flowing feeling of warmth and concern for someone else's well-being, bringing an urge to support or look out for them.
- **Disgust:** The strong, rejecting feeling that makes you want to pull back, close off, or push something away because it feels gross, toxic, or wrong.
- **Attraction:** The magnetic pull or urge to move closer to someone or something, whether that pull is physical, emotional, or mental.

**Locate the feeling.** For any score above zero, pinpoint exactly where the sensation sits in your body—the jaw, the chest, the stomach, the throat, and the shoulders are the usual places. Two or three emotions registering above 0 at once is normal; blends are where most of the feelings that have no clean name actually live.

**Check your physical baseline.** Check three physical states: tired, hungry, and in pain. These physical states are frequently mistaken for emotions. A high score on anger at four in the afternoon that turns out to be hunger is a common and useful discovery.

**Record your data (optional).** If you find it helpful, keep a quick note in a journal, a spreadsheet, or a notepad app on your phone with the date, the time, the words above 0 with their numbers, and where you felt them. If nothing is there, write down that nothing was there. A flat check-in is data, not a failed attempt.

For the first few weeks, rely entirely on the alarms to prompt you. Eventually—usually around week three—the words will begin arriving on their own. You might be mid-conversation and suddenly realize you are feeling guilt without having run through the list.

When this spontaneous recognition starts, begin naming your emotions exactly as they happen. When an emotion surfaces, label it in the moment—out loud if you have the privacy, or silently if you do not. If it is a blend, name the blend. Acknowledging, "This is anger, and underneath it is fear," is much more useful than forcing a single label.

Keep the scheduled alarms running even after this shift happens. The daily check-ins are the physical maintenance that keeps the vocabulary accessible; catching your feelings in real time is simply the reward for doing the maintenance.

### The Body Scan

The body scan is the second practice. Here, you move your attention slowly through your body to notice what is physically present without trying to change any of it. Lie on your back somewhere you will not be interrupted, or sit upright if lying down puts you to sleep, and close your eyes.

Using a guided audio track, such as the [3-Minute Body Scan](https://www.uclahealth.org/uclamindful/guided-meditations) from UCLA's Mindful Awareness Research Center, is much easier than working from memory at the start.

Begin at your feet and work upward through the lower legs, knees, thighs, hips, lower back, stomach, chest, upper back, shoulders, arms, hands, neck, throat, jaw, face, and scalp. Rest your attention on each area for a few breaths. Notice whatever is there—warmth, pressure, tightness, aching, tingling—and notice when there is nothing at all. Some regions may stay blank for weeks, which is a normal finding rather than a problem.

Your mind will naturally wander. That is not a failure; noticing where your mind went and bringing it back to the body is the practice itself. Start with just three minutes a day. You can gradually work your way up to ten minutes as you get more comfortable. While these sessions can eventually extend to thirty minutes or more, that is not required. Doing it consistently matters; once a day for a few minutes is plenty to build the foundation.

### The Partner Check-In

In the third practice, you and another person take turns exploring your emotional and physical states aloud. The entire exercise takes about ten to twelve minutes. Sit facing each other, agree in advance who will speak first, and set a timer for about two and a half minutes per answer so that neither of you is watching the clock.

The listener asks two specific questions in sequence. After asking a question, the listener remains completely silent—offering no responses, no reassurance, and no fixing—until the speaker is finished answering.

> "Describe a situation within the last 24 hours in which you experienced a difficult emotion, and how did it feel in your body?"

The listener thanks the speaker, then asks the second question:

> "Describe a situation within the last 24 hours in which you felt grateful, and how did that feel in your body?"

Once the speaker finishes answering the second question, the listener thanks them again, and the roles swap.

This practice improves your ability to put feelings into words more than your ability to identify them, which is why it works alongside the solo check-in rather than replacing it ([Silveira and colleagues, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165032723010704)). If the exercise turns into a conversation about a conflict between the two of you, stop and come back to it another time. This is not the place to work on the relationship.

### A Note on Attention

Paying closer attention to your internal state is not automatically better for everyone. Turning up your awareness to what you feel when there is nobody around to help you carry it can occasionally leave you feeling overwhelmed ([Goldman and colleagues, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39449575/)). If starting these practices makes you feel worse, and it stays that way for weeks, that is information about your current circumstances rather than a failure on your part, and it is a reason to get support in place before you continue.
