The Overview
Attention is the ability to put your mind on something and keep it there while other things pull at it. Not to think about it well—just to stay on it.

Emotions are information. They are physical signals telling you something is going on and pushing you to act—lights on a dashboard. But a dashboard only helps someone who actually looks at it. If you cannot hold your mind on your own chest for a few seconds, you will not catch the heat arriving there. The first time you find out you are angry will be when you hear your own raised voice, and by then, you are already acting on it. Attention buys you the seconds in between. Every other skill in this course runs on that gap.

Attention wanders. That is not a problem you can fix. Most people quit meditating because they cannot stay focused, and they read the wandering as proof they are bad at it. The wandering is the exercise. The skill is the return: noticing you have drifted, and coming back without making a thing of it. Every drift is another repetition.

Think about shooting a basketball. The best players in the world miss about half their shots, and they never stop missing. What they have is a fast, quiet feedback loop: that one was long, that one drifted left, adjust, shoot again. Nobody watches a professional miss and concludes they cannot shoot.

Attention works the same way. When experienced meditators with over a thousand hours of practice were asked to watch their breath for twenty minutes in a brain scanner, they still lost focus about fifteen times—roughly once every eighty seconds (Hasenkamp and colleagues, 2012). These were not beginners, and they still wandered.

You will not make every shot. But you can make far more than you make now. Practice moves two things: you drift less often, and you notice sooner when you do (Mrazek and colleagues, 2013; Hasenkamp and colleagues, 2012). Noticing sooner does the heavier work because it shortens every episode. The two compound—fewer trips, and less time gone on each one. What you get is not a permanently quiet mind. It is far more of your day spent exactly where you meant to be.

That is worth having by itself. Minds are somewhere other than the task about 47% of the time, and people are less happy when theirs is—during every activity measured (Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010). Pleasant daydreams did not beat simply being absorbed in what you were doing. No kind of drifting did.

The amounts required to change this are smaller than most people picture. Thirteen minutes a day changed attention, memory, and mood in people who had never meditated—at eight weeks, though not yet at four (Basso and colleagues, 2019). One minute a day is a real place to start. And sessions can be seconds long. The teacher Yongey Mingyur Rinpoche advises short times, many times—one intentional breath, many times a day, waiting for the kettle or before you open your laptop. That instruction comes from a teaching tradition rather than from trials, and I pass it on because the mechanism is the same, and because a small practice you keep beats a serious one you quit.

One caution belongs here up front. Attention practice makes you notice more of what you feel, but noticing is not the part that helps you. Allowing what you find is the active ingredient, and that is a separate skill (Lindsay and colleagues, 2018). For some people, turning the volume up surfaces more than they can hold on their own—grief, fear, or a sense of being outside their own life. Observation is where allowing gets trained, and it is the better place to begin if the habit of leaving your body is old and familiar.

For everything else, attention is the prerequisite. Attention you can place on purpose is attention you can spend on the work in front of you, on the person talking to you, and on the physical signals inside your own body.
