## The Evidence

**People who can tell their unpleasant feelings apart are less aggressive on the days they are angriest.** Three separate studies followed college students through several weeks of daily diaries, each day recording both their emotions and their behavior. Researchers scored each person on how finely they told their negative emotions apart — whether their answers actually moved with what was happening to them that day, rather than describing every bad day the same way — then compared people who scored high on that measure to people who scored low, on the specific days each person was angriest. On those days, the people who told their feelings apart most precisely acted less aggressively than the people who didn't: 17% less in the first study, 43% less in the second, and 16% less in the third ([Pond and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22023359/)). Nobody was taught anything here — this compares people who already differ, not the effect of a practice.

**Being unable to name your feelings leads to loneliness, and to a less satisfying marriage.** Researchers surveyed 216 people from 115 couples on how well they could name their feelings, how supported they felt, how lonely they were, and how satisfied they were in their marriage. The harder someone found it to name what they felt, the lonelier they were; and the lonelier they were, the less satisfied with their marriage ([Panahi and colleagues, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6259507/)). A separate study of 155 couples traced the same route with one more step in it: the harder it was to name feelings, the lonelier the person; the lonelier the person, the less they confided in their spouse; and the less they confided, the worse the marriage — for husbands and wives alike ([Frye-Cox and Hesse, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23544920/)).

**People who struggle to identify their feelings harm themselves more.** Researchers pooled 23 studies covering 8,724 people. The questionnaire they all used separates trouble identifying what you feel from trouble putting words to it, and people with more of either harmed themselves more. It also measures a thinking style aimed at outside events rather than inner ones, and that made no difference. The pattern was stronger among teenagers than among adults. One study inside the set followed 566 teenagers over time: those who had the most trouble naming their feelings at the start were the ones harming themselves five months later ([Norman and colleagues, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sjop.12668)).

**People who struggle to identify their feelings think about suicide more.** Researchers pooled 34 studies covering 10,104 adults, measuring two separate things: how much someone thinks about suicide, and whether they have attempted it. The link to thinking about suicide was strong, and it held up when researchers accounted for age, gender, and how depressed someone was. The link to attempting was much weaker, and under that same scrutiny it did not hold up — other factors accounted for it ([Hemming and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599888/)).

**People who struggle to identify their feelings drink and use drugs more.** Researchers pooled 52 studies covering 22,712 people, comparing those with substance problems against those without. Substance users had more trouble identifying what they felt and putting words to it. How big the difference was depended a great deal on who was being studied: it was largest among people in treatment for a substance problem, smaller in the general population, and smallest among college students. It was also about twice as large in samples that were mostly men ([Honkalampi and colleagues, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sjop.12821)).

**Anger you carry, and anger you let out, both show up in the heart.** Researchers pooled 25 studies that followed initially healthy people over time, measuring their anger and hostility and then tracking who developed coronary heart disease. People with higher anger and hostility were about a fifth more likely to develop heart disease over the following years ([Chida and Steptoe, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2008.11.044)). Separately, nearly 4,000 people hospitalized for a heart attack were asked whether they had experienced an angry outburst in the two hours before it started, and compared that to how often they were angry at other times. The risk of a heart attack was roughly two and a half times higher in the two hours after an outburst, and it rose further the more intense the anger was ([Mostofsky and colleagues, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3753402/)). Both findings are about anger itself — not about whether noticing the anger changes anything — but they show what an unexamined feeling does in the body.

**People who have the most trouble telling a feeling from a bodily sensation report the most physical symptoms.** Researchers pooled 18 studies measuring how well people could identify their emotions alongside how many physical symptoms they reported. People who struggled more to name their feelings reported more symptoms ([De Gucht and Heiser, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12726898/)). The link was strongest for distinguishing feelings from bodily sensations — precisely the confusion the lesson describes. The evidence is correlational, so it cannot establish that difficulty identifying feelings causes more symptom reporting.

**People who cannot identify their feelings lose social support and feel more sad and fearful.** Researchers surveyed 667 Australian high school students in Grade 8 and again in Grade 9, measuring their ability to identify emotions, the quality and quantity of their social support, and their positive and negative affect. Students who started with lower emotion identification skill ended the year with less social support, less positive affect, and more fear ([Ciarrochi and colleagues, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adolescence.2007.10.004)). Among boys, low identification also predicted increases in sadness.

**People who know their own feelings tend to read other people better.** Researchers measured how discriminatingly people used emotion words — not how many words they knew, but whether their ratings of the same photographs changed from one picture to the next — then tested their ability to read emotions in three ways: from faces alone, from faces with body and voice, and from the eyes only. People who told their own feelings apart more precisely were better at reading other people on all three tasks, and the result replicated across two studies with different populations ([Israelashvili and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30734635/)). The direction is not established — it could be that reading yourself helps you read others, or that reading others sharpens your own vocabulary, or both.

**Low differentiation doesn't change which strategies people reach for — it makes the strategies work less well.** Researchers tracked people through their daily lives, measuring both how finely they told their emotions apart and which strategies they used when distressed. People who struggled to distinguish their feelings did not reach for worse strategies than anyone else — they used the same tools. But those tools worked less well: among low differentiators, the strategies were more strongly associated with increased negative emotion in both studies ([Kalokerinos and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30990768/)).

**People who can tell whether they feel anxious, ashamed, or angry — instead of just "bad" — are less likely to act aggressively, binge eat, relapse into substance use, skip treatment, or self-injure.** Researchers pooled 17 studies covering 2,182 people, combining those behaviors into a single measure of maladaptive responding. People who told their negative emotions apart more precisely showed less of that pooled pattern, and the link held after accounting for how much negative emotion they felt overall — so it was not simply that less distressed people differentiate better ([Seah and Coifman, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000968)). The result did not depend on whether the samples were clinical or community. The entire evidence base is correlational, and the single self-injury study inside the pool was not significant on its own.

**Of everything measured, noticing what you feel is the weakest predictor of how you behave.** Researchers pooled 137 studies covering 252,605 people, comparing six facets of emotion regulation against aggressive behavior. Emotional awareness — noticing what you feel — had the smallest correlation with aggression of all six facets, and four of the others (impulse control, access to strategies, non-acceptance, and goal-directed behavior) were each significantly stronger predictors ([Smith and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/ab.70055)). The effect was consistent rather than noisy, but awareness came first in the curriculum precisely because it is the foundation other skills build on, not because it is the strongest predictor on its own.

**Six weeks of rating named emotions several times a day improves the ability to tell unpleasant feelings apart.** Researchers randomly assigned 79 adults with diagnosed depression to either six weeks of rating ten named emotions ten times a day, three days a week, or to a control group. The practice was the only intervention — no teaching, no vocabulary instruction, no therapy content. People who rated their emotions became better at telling their negative feelings apart; the control group did not change ([Widdershoven and colleagues, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2019.09.074)). Positive emotions showed an upward trend, but the study was too small to detect it reliably. There was no dose-response — people who completed more ratings did not improve more than people who completed fewer.
