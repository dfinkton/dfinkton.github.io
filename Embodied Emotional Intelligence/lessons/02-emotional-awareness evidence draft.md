## The Evidence

### What Happens Without Awareness

**People who can tell their unpleasant feelings apart are less aggressive on the days they are angriest.** Three separate studies followed college students through several weeks of daily diaries, each day recording both their emotions and their behavior. Researchers scored each person on how finely they told their negative emotions apart — whether their answers actually moved with what was happening to them that day, rather than describing every bad day the same way — then compared people who scored high on that measure to people who scored low, on the specific days each person was angriest. On those days, the people who told their feelings apart most precisely acted less aggressively than the people who didn't: 17% less in the first study, 43% less in the second, and 16% less in the third ([Pond and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22023359/)). Nobody was taught anything here — this compares people who already differ, not the effect of a practice.

**Being unable to name your feelings leads to loneliness, and to a less satisfying marriage.** Researchers surveyed 216 people from 115 couples on how well they could name their feelings, how supported they felt, how lonely they were, and how satisfied they were in their marriage. The harder someone found it to name what they felt, the lonelier they were; and the lonelier they were, the less satisfied with their marriage ([Panahi and colleagues, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6259507/)). A separate study of 155 couples traced the same route with one more step in it: the harder it was to name feelings, the lonelier the person; the lonelier the person, the less they confided in their spouse; and the less they confided, the worse the marriage — for husbands and wives alike ([Frye-Cox and Hesse, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23544920/)).

**People who struggle to identify their feelings harm themselves more.** Researchers pooled 23 studies covering 8,724 people. The questionnaire they all used separates trouble identifying what you feel from trouble putting words to it, and people with more of either harmed themselves more. It also measures a thinking style aimed at outside events rather than inner ones, and that made no difference. The pattern was stronger among teenagers than among adults. One study inside the set followed 566 teenagers over time: those who had the most trouble naming their feelings at the start were the ones harming themselves five months later ([Norman and colleagues, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sjop.12668)).

**People who struggle to identify their feelings think about suicide more.** Researchers pooled 34 studies covering 10,104 adults, measuring two separate things: how much someone thinks about suicide, and whether they have attempted it. The link to thinking about suicide was strong, and it held up when researchers accounted for age, gender, and how depressed someone was. The link to attempting was much weaker, and under that same scrutiny it did not hold up — other factors accounted for it ([Hemming and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599888/)).

**People who struggle to identify their feelings drink and use drugs more.** Researchers pooled 52 studies covering 22,712 people, comparing those with substance problems against those without. Substance users had more trouble identifying what they felt and putting words to it. How big the difference was depended a great deal on who was being studied: it was largest among people in treatment for a substance problem, smaller in the general population, and smallest among college students. It was also about twice as large in samples that were mostly men ([Honkalampi and colleagues, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sjop.12821)).

**Anger you carry, and anger you let out, both show up in the heart.** Researchers pooled 25 studies that followed initially healthy people over time, measuring their anger and hostility and then tracking who developed coronary heart disease. People with higher anger and hostility were about a fifth more likely to develop heart disease over the following years ([Chida and Steptoe, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2008.11.044)). Separately, nearly 4,000 people hospitalized for a heart attack were asked whether they had experienced an angry outburst in the two hours before it started, and compared that to how often they were angry at other times. The risk of a heart attack was roughly two and a half times higher in the two hours after an outburst, and it rose further the more intense the anger was ([Mostofsky and colleagues, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3753402/)). Both findings are about anger itself — not about whether noticing the anger changes anything — but they show what an unexamined feeling does in the body.

**People who have the most trouble telling a feeling from a bodily sensation report the most physical symptoms.** Researchers pooled 18 studies measuring how well people could identify their emotions alongside how many physical symptoms they reported. People who struggled more to name their feelings reported more symptoms ([De Gucht and Heiser, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12726898/)). The link was strongest for distinguishing feelings from bodily sensations — precisely the confusion the lesson describes. The evidence is correlational, so it cannot establish that difficulty identifying feelings causes more symptom reporting.

**People who cannot identify their feelings lose social support and feel more sad and fearful.** Researchers surveyed 667 Australian high school students in Grade 8 and again in Grade 9, measuring their ability to identify emotions, the quality and quantity of their social support, and their positive and negative affect. Students who started with lower emotion identification skill ended the year with less social support, less positive affect, and more fear ([Ciarrochi and colleagues, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adolescence.2007.10.004)). Among boys, low identification also predicted increases in sadness.

### What Awareness Enables

**People who know their own feelings tend to read other people better.** Researchers measured how precisely people could tell their own feelings apart — by having them rate emotionally charged photographs using the same set of emotion words, then checking whether their ratings shifted from one picture to the next. They then tested people's ability to read emotions in three ways: from faces alone, from faces with body and voice, and from the eyes only. People who told their own feelings apart more precisely were better at reading other people on all three tasks, and the result replicated across two studies with different populations ([Israelashvili and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30734635/)). The direction is not established — it could be that reading yourself helps you read others, or that reading others sharpens your own vocabulary, or both.

**People who can't tell their feelings apart don't use worse strategies — but the strategies don't work as well for them.** Researchers tracked people through their daily lives, measuring both how finely they told their emotions apart and which strategies they used when distressed. People who struggled to distinguish their feelings used the same strategies as everyone else — reappraisal, distraction, social sharing, and so on. But those strategies didn't reduce their negative emotion as much. The study doesn't explain why, only that it happens ([Kalokerinos and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30990768/)).

**People who can tell whether they feel anxious, ashamed, or angry — instead of just "bad" — are less likely to act aggressively, binge eat, relapse into substance use, skip treatment, or self-injure.** Researchers pooled 17 studies covering 2,182 people, looking at those behaviors together. People who told their negative emotions apart more precisely had fewer of those problems, and the link held even after accounting for how much negative emotion they felt overall — so it wasn't simply that less distressed people differentiate better ([Seah and Coifman, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000968)). The result was the same whether the samples came from clinical or community populations. The entire evidence base is correlational, and the self-injury finding wasn't significant when examined on its own.

**Emotional awareness alone has a small impact on aggression — it is a foundational skill, not the destination.** Researchers pooled 137 studies covering 252,605 people, examining how six facets of emotion regulation relate to aggressive behavior: impulse control, access to strategies, goal-directed behavior, non-acceptance of emotions, emotional clarity, and emotional awareness. Emotional awareness had the weakest association with aggression of all six. Impulse control, access to strategies, and the ability to direct your behavior all mattered more. Awareness is the starting point, not the thing that changes behavior on its own ([Smith and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/ab.70055)).

### What the Practices Do

**After six weeks of rating their emotions several times a day, people were better able to tell unpleasant feelings apart — no longer just saying "I feel bad."** Researchers randomly assigned 79 adults with diagnosed depression to either six weeks of rating ten named emotions ten times a day, three days a week, or to a control group. The practice was the only intervention — no teaching, no vocabulary instruction, no therapy content. People who rated their emotions became better at telling their negative feelings apart; the control group did not change ([Widdershoven and colleagues, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2019.09.074)). Positive emotions showed an upward trend, but the study was too small to detect it reliably. There was no dose-response — people who completed more ratings did not improve more than people who completed fewer.

**Naming fear out loud while you feel it leaves you calmer when you face future fears.** Researchers randomly assigned 88 spider-fearful adults to one of four conditions during live exposure to a real spider: describe your fear in emotion words, describe the spider in neutral words, talk about your house, or exposure with no instruction. A week later, when exposed to a different spider in a different room, the people who had named their fear showed a larger drop in skin conductance than the other groups — and the more fear words they used, the greater the drop ([Kircanski and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22902637/)). There was no significant difference in self-reported fear at any timepoint — the body carried the effect, not the conscious sense of feeling better.

**Three months of body scan improves your ability to read the body for what you're feeling.** Researchers randomly assigned 148 healthy adults to either practice body scan and breath meditation for 13 weeks, or to a control group that was tested again without any training. The practice group did 20 minutes of body scan plus 10 minutes of breath meditation five days a week, plus weekly two-hour classes. After three months, they were better at connecting body sensations to emotional states — the people who liked the practice and integrated it into daily life improved the most ([Bornemann and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01504)).

**A compassion-focused, partner-sharing practice lowered the body's actual hormonal stress response, not just how stressed people said they felt.** Researchers trained 313 healthy adults in three different types of mental practice over nine months. One group practiced paying attention to their body and breath. Another group practiced compassion and shared their emotional experiences with a partner. A third group practiced taking other people's perspectives. All three types made people feel less stressed when tested in a lab stress situation. Only the compassion and partner-sharing practice actually changed the body's stress response — cortisol levels dropped by up to 51%. The attention and body-scan practice only changed how people reported feeling, not what their bodies were doing ([Engert and colleagues, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1700495)).

**Sharing emotional experiences with a partner for just 12 minutes a day helped people improve their ability to put their feelings into words.** Researchers assigned 285 adults to one of two practices: either twelve minutes daily of solo mindfulness training, or twelve minutes daily with a partner where you take turns describing a difficult emotion you felt in the last 24 hours and how it showed up in your body, then describing something you felt grateful for and how that felt in your body. After ten weeks, both groups improved at describing their feelings compared to a waitlist control. The partner practice showed larger improvements — people got better at putting words to what they were feeling. The partner group was also more likely to drop out, with 25% leaving compared to 15% in the solo group ([Silveira and colleagues, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2023.08.113)).

**Your body has a consistent map for each emotion.** Researchers asked 701 participants to color on a body outline where they felt activity increasing and decreasing when given emotion words, stories, film clips, or faces. The patterns were distinct enough for each emotion that a computer could identify which emotion someone was feeling just from their coloring — well above chance. The maps were consistent across Finnish, Swedish, and Taiwanese participants ([Nummenmaa and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1321662111)).

### The Limits

**Emotional awareness makes you more sensitive to the support around you — and that can help or hurt.** Researchers followed 460 women with breast cancer over one year. Women with higher emotional awareness and low social support had greater depression and lower optimism. Higher awareness predicted greater depression at baseline, but lower depression at nine-month follow-up. The authors read this as awareness increasing sensitivity to whatever support is available — when support is low, that sensitivity makes things worse ([Goldman and colleagues, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39449575/)).

**Stress makes it harder to tell your feelings apart.** Researchers tracked 200 first-year students at three time points over a year, measuring their daily stress and their ability to distinguish between different negative emotions. Stress on one day predicted lower ability to tell negative emotions apart the next day, even after accounting for that day's own stress levels. The effect was specific to stress — anger, anxiety, sadness, depression, and loneliness all correlated with differentiation on the same day, but none predicted it forward the way stress did ([Erbas and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000126)).

**How aware people say they are of their emotions and how well they can actually describe them are only weakly related.** Researchers combined 28 samples covering 2,857 people, comparing two measures. The first (TAS-20) is a questionnaire asking people to rate statements like "I am often confused about what emotion I am feeling" — it measures how aware people believe they are. The second (LEAS) asks people to write responses to emotional scenarios, which trained raters then score for how many distinct emotions they identify and how specifically they describe them — it measures verbal fluency about emotions. The two showed only a weak relationship. The authors concluded they measure different aspects of emotional functioning, not the same thing ([Maroti and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00780)).
