## The Evidence

### How the Skill Develops

**Young children's emotion categories are real but far too wide.** [Widen and Russell, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2008.01.002) tested 168 children aged 2–5, plus 24 adults as a ceiling, on two tasks: freely labeling six facial expressions, then sorting faces into a labeled box. Categories start organized mainly by pleasant-versus-unpleasant and narrow gradually. The order in which children spontaneously produce labels was consistent: happiness alone first (mean 30.2 months), then happiness plus anger or sadness (35.4–35.7mo), then all three (41.2mo), then fear or surprise (50.5mo), then the other of those two (53.1mo), with disgust last — only 7 of 168 children got that far. 81% of children fit that exact sequence. Worth knowing: the authors correct their own earlier (2003) claim that later-emerging categories start out narrower. That turned out to be an artifact of the labeling task, where well-practiced early words get over-used when a child is unsure. On the sorting task, which lacks that bias, all four tested categories were equally broad at first and narrowed at similar rates.

**Differentiation follows a U-shape and bottoms out in adolescence.** [Nook and colleagues, 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0956797618773357) sampled 143 people aged 5.78–25.91. Emotion differentiation was relatively high in childhood, dropped through adolescence, and rose again into adulthood — a quadratic effect, β=0.32, p<.001, with a low point around age 15.7. The dip was mediated by young children's tendency to report one emotion at a time rather than several at once, and was not explained by intensity differences or careless responding. One caution about reading the childhood end: reporting a single emotion scores as high differentiation on this measure while a child's categories are still very wide, so this is not evidence that five-year-olds are better at this than fifteen-year-olds.

**It keeps improving across adulthood, well past middle age.** [Carstensen and colleagues, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.4.644) had 184 adults aged 18–94 (31% African American) rate 19 emotion terms five times a day for a week. Differentiation correlated with age at r=.28, p<.01 — linear, with no falloff at the top end — and held across ethnicity, gender and socioeconomic status, and was not explained by personality, health, or verbal fluency. Two other findings from the same data: negative emotion became less frequent from 18 to about 60 and then stopped declining, and the degree to which pleasant and unpleasant feelings showed up *in the same moment* became reliably more common with age (under 60, the average within-person correlation was −.42; over 60, −.25). Cross-sectional, which the authors flag directly: claims about age *change* from a cross-sectional design "must be tempered accordingly."

### What It Costs

**Anxiety — the best-established link, from two different constructs.** [Yeung and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/cps0000288) is a PRISMA-registered meta-analysis of 69 studies where every clinical diagnosis was confirmed with a standardized interview. Anxiety-disorder groups scored higher than controls on total alexithymia at SMD=0.97 [0.64, 1.30] — a large effect — driven by difficulty identifying feelings (0.96) and describing them (0.44), with externally oriented thinking not significant (0.07). Across the three disorder families they studied, anxiety showed the *strongest* correlation between symptom severity and alexithymia: r=.39, ahead of eating disorders (.37) and depression (.32), and with unusually low heterogeneity for this literature (I²=21.5%). **The caveat that matters:** their anxiety data is overwhelmingly panic disorder — 12 of roughly 21 anxiety groups, with only 4 social anxiety and 2 generalized anxiety. The authors flag this themselves as a gap in the field. Narrowed to panic disorder alone, the effect is moderate rather than large (SMD=0.77). For anxiety in the everyday sense, the better source is [Kashdan and Farmer, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4191833/): 43 adults with SCID-diagnosed generalized social anxiety disorder against 42 controls, 14 days of experience sampling. Negative-emotion differentiation was worse in the clinical group during random prompts (d=0.63) and during social interactions (d=0.60), and it survived controlling for emotion intensity, emotion variability, and comorbid depression and anxiety diagnoses. Positive-emotion differentiation showed no group difference — the same asymmetry found independently by Barrett 2001 and Demiralp 2012.

**Suicidal thoughts, and why they separate from suicidal acts.** [Hemming and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599888/) pooled 34 studies and 10,104 adults. Alexithymia correlated with suicidal *ideation* at r=0.54 [0.40, 0.65] and with suicidal *behaviour* at r=0.25 [0.16, 0.34]. The subscale pattern held for behaviour in the usual way (DIF 0.16, DDF 0.15, EOT 0.00, ns) but ideation is the one place in this whole literature where externally oriented thinking was *not* null (r=0.28). Two things temper the headline number. Six studies from a single Italian research group drove a substantial part of it — removing that cluster drops the ideation effect from 0.54 to 0.41, still moderate. And on the behaviour side the authors' own multivariate check led them to conclude outright that "the relationship between suicide behaviours and alexithymia is not robust" once other variables are accounted for. Heterogeneity was severe throughout (I²=95% for ideation), and neither age nor Eastern-versus-Western location explained any of it.

**Depression — replicated across three independent meta-analyses, with a real spread.** [Liu, He and Hou, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1465286/full) pooled 35 studies and 23,085 people: total alexithymia with depression r=0.455 [0.417, 0.491], stable under their own sensitivity analyses, with no detected publication bias and invariance to which measurement instrument was used. [Li and colleagues, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25769520/), an earlier and independent Western-only analysis of 19 studies and 3,572 people, found the same pattern: medium relationships for total score, difficulty identifying feelings, and difficulty describing feelings; a weak one for externally oriented thinking. Yeung and colleagues, 2026 gets a third, slightly lower number (r=.32). So the direction and the subscale pattern replicate cleanly; the exact coefficient moves between roughly .32 and .46 depending on which analysis you read, which is worth knowing before treating any one decimal as settled. One further caution specific to the largest of the three: the subscale breakdown in Liu et al. rests on 30 studies and 7,885 participants, not the full 23,085, and the paper carries enough internal errors — including a citation to a political-science paper on Quebec sovereignty in support of a cross-cultural claim, and an impossible sample size in its structural model — that the replication matters more than the paper does.

**Eating disorders show the largest group differences in this literature.** In [Yeung and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/cps0000288), eating-disorder groups exceeded controls at SMD=1.29 overall, with anorexia nervosa at 1.69 and bulimia nervosa at 1.22 — the largest single figures anywhere in this evidence base. Note the asymmetry, though: the *group difference* is very large while the correlation between eating-disorder symptom severity and alexithymia was not statistically significant (r=.29, CI crossing zero, from only 5 studies). So people with these diagnoses differ markedly from people without them; whether more severe symptoms go with more alexithymia is not established here.

**Sleep.** [Alimoradi and colleagues, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34958823/) pooled 26 studies across 12 countries, 7,546 participants: overall association r=0.44 [0.31, 0.56], larger in patient populations (0.55) than healthy ones (0.30) — the same clinical-versus-general gradient that shows up throughout this literature.

**Physical symptoms.** [La Touche and colleagues, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8638568/) pooled 10 studies of craniofacial pain — migraine in six, tension-type headache in two, temporomandibular disorders in two — covering 1,228 patients: total alexithymia SMD=0.46 [0.22, 0.71] versus controls. The subscale pattern breaks from the usual here, with difficulty identifying feelings significant (0.39) but describing feelings not (0.15, ns). [Ismaiel and colleagues, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11474551/) found irritable bowel syndrome patients scored 8.06 points higher on the TAS-20 than healthy controls [2.55, 13.57] — but **no significant difference between IBS and inflammatory bowel disease**, which is an organic autoimmune condition rather than a functional one. If the mechanism were emotional distress being misread as gut symptoms in the absence of real pathology, IBS should have exceeded IBD. It didn't, in the two studies available. [De Gucht and Heiser, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12726898/) found a small-to-moderate relationship between alexithymia and somatic symptom reporting, strongest for difficulty identifying feelings, with externally oriented thinking "virtually unrelated" — and state the limitation that decides how much weight this can carry: symptom questionnaires "can only check for symptoms, not whether these symptoms are medically explained." Only one prospective study existed at the time of their review. Reverse causation is unusually live across this whole cluster: being chronically ill plausibly changes how a person attends to and reports their feelings.

**Blood pressure — included here because it is too weak for the lesson body.** [Di Tella and colleagues, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10307708/) found alexithymia prevalence of 26.3% in people with hypertension against 15.0% without, pooled odds ratio 3.15 — but the confidence interval runs [1.14, 8.74], from only five studies, with I²=87.5%. Their meta-regression also found alexithymia prevalence *declining* in more recent studies, which raises the possibility of a measurement-era artifact. The authors state they cannot address whether hypertension or its treatment might change alexithymia scores rather than the reverse. Real enough to note; too fragile to build on.

**The behavioural costs.** [Pond and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22023359/) ran three daily-diary studies with 628 undergraduates: high differentiators showed 17% fewer aggressive tendencies on high-anger days in the first study, 43% less aggression when provoked and angry in the second, and 16% less when angry in the third, with the effect partially mediated by daily emotional control. The authors state directly that the findings "may not reveal causal relationships." [Honkalampi and colleagues, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sjop.12821) pooled 52 studies and 22,712 people on substance use: overall d=0.62 [0.49, 0.76], with difficulty identifying feelings again carrying it (0.64) over describing (0.44) and external orientation (0.19). Population mattered a great deal — clinical samples d=0.83, general population 0.51, students 0.28 — as did sex, with majority-male samples at 0.85 against 0.44. Only 2 of the 52 studies were longitudinal. [Norman and colleagues, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sjop.12668) pooled 23 studies and 8,724 people on self-harm: g=0.57 [0.46, 0.69], publication bias formally ruled out, driven by DIF (0.61) and DDF (0.41) with EOT not significant (0.10). The effect was significantly larger in adolescents (g=0.69) than adults (0.48), p=.035. [Seah and Coifman, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000968) pooled 17 samples and 2,182 people across binge eating, aggression, substance relapse, treatment non-adherence and self-injury as one class: r=−.15 [−.21, −.09]. Small, but it survived controlling for mean negative affect and — importantly for whether any of this applies outside clinical populations — **did not depend on clinical status at all** (Q=.01, p=.91). One correction worth recording: this paper is sometimes cited for self-injury specifically. It shouldn't be. The single self-injury study inside it was not independently significant (r=−.09, ns).

**Relationships.** [Humphreys, Wood and Parker, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2008.09.002) is the best-controlled source here: 158 undergraduates in relationships of at least three months, with a structural model that included a negative-affect control specifically to rule out the "these people are just in a bad mood" explanation. Alexithymia predicted relationship satisfaction at β=−0.52 and sexual satisfaction at β=−0.51, while negative affect's own paths to both were non-significant. [Besharat and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.5539/ijps.v6n3p106) is the largest sample in this cluster, 688 married Iranian university students: r=−.49 for women and −.45 for men, with avoidant attachment significantly moderating the relationship. [Panahi and colleagues, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6259507/) found something more specific in 108 Iranian couples: the direct path from alexithymia to marital satisfaction was **not** significant and was dropped from the model, with the entire effect running through loneliness. Four independent samples across three countries converge on roughly −.45 to −.55, all cross-sectional.

**Reading other people — and a correction to a common belief.** [Israelashvili and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30734635/) ran two studies, 363 and 217 participants: people who differentiated their own negative emotions more finely were *more* accurate at recognizing others' facial expressions, replicated across recognition tasks that varied how much emotional information was available. [Grynberg and colleagues, 2012](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0042429) reviewed 24 studies and found the same direction, with one especially useful control: in the seven clinical studies that tested it, diagnostic-group differences in face-reading **disappeared once alexithymia was accounted for** — suggesting it is the alexithymia travelling with a diagnosis, not the diagnosis, that predicts poor face-reading. Deficits appeared across all six basic emotions and were worse for degraded or briefly-presented faces. The authors are explicit that **none of the studies reported effect sizes**, so this literature establishes direction and breadth but not magnitude, and they caution it would be "premature" to conclude any particular emotion is specifically affected. Together these two undercut the folk idea that people out of touch with themselves are unusually perceptive about everyone else. On reading a *partner* specifically, [Erbas and colleagues, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550616633504) is narrower than it is usually made out to be: across 49 Belgian couples doing 7 days of experience sampling, negative-emotion differentiation correlated with accuracy about a partner's *valence* at r=.30 — but was null for arousal accuracy and null for tracking a partner's mood *changes* over time. The authors state that "it is not possible to draw conclusions on the direction of the relationship." Vocal tone is a separate and genuinely contested question: no meta-analysis exists, and the most-cited primary study found no difference between high- and low-alexithymia groups on affective prosody.

### Which Came First

**Most of this literature watches people rather than changing anything.** [Demiralp and colleagues, 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797612444903) illustrates why direction stays open: 53 people with SCID-diagnosed major depression and 53 matched controls did 7–8 days of experience sampling. The depressed group showed lower negative-emotion differentiation, F(1,98)=7.18, d=−0.54, and the effect survived controlling for emotional intensity and variability — so it isn't simply that depressed people feel worse or more variably. But cause and consequence can't be separated in that design.

**Two studies did establish which came first, and both point the same way.** [Weissman and colleagues, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33758688/) followed 259 children and adolescents aged 8–16 for about 22 months, in a sample recruited specifically for variation in violence exposure. Low emotional awareness predicted general psychopathology at follow-up while controlling for psychopathology at baseline (B=.019, p=.011) — that is, it predicted *change*, not merely co-occurrence. It also mediated the path from violence exposure to worsening symptoms, with a bootstrapped indirect effect of .07 [.01, .15]. Violence-exposed youth had worse emotional awareness to begin with (d=.59), robust to controls for income and racial minority status. Their first study (N=120) found low emotional awareness correlated with general psychopathology at r=.49, and with anxiety specifically at r=.62 — the strongest correlation in their table. One nuance most summaries drop: in both studies, emotional awareness worsened with age in girls but not boys, and the age-related mediation held only for females. Separately, the single longitudinal study inside [Norman and colleagues, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/sjop.12668) (Garisch and Wilson, 2015, following 566 adolescents) found alexithymia predicted self-harm five months later rather than the reverse — the only temporal-precedence evidence in that literature, in either direction.

**And it moves with circumstance.** [Erbas and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000126) followed 200 first-year students across three measurement bursts over a year. Stress on one day predicted lower negative-emotion differentiation the *next* day (p=.010), holding after controlling for that day's own stress. A robustness check ruled out the obvious confound: anger, anxiety, sadness, depression and loneliness all predicted differentiation concurrently, but none of them predicted it prospectively the way stress did. Worth knowing that the authors resist framing this as malfunction — no well-being measure consistently moderated the effect, and they argue collapsing everything into good-or-bad while handling something hard may be an adaptive simplification rather than a failure.

### Uncertainties and Opposing Views

**The field's own methodological critique is the most important caveat here.** [Thompson, Springstein and Boden, 2021](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spc3.12584) reviewed 73 articles covering 82 studies. Emotion differentiation is measured five different ways and the methods barely agree with each other — a photo-based differentiation task and experience-sampling differentiation correlate at only about r=.22, "suggesting they measure somewhat different constructs." Worse, the textbook definition and the standard statistic contradict each other: a person who says "I was angry, worried, and disappointed" is described in every example as *high* differentiation, but the usual scoring would likely rate that moment as *low*, because those three ratings covary. The authors give that exact example. Their recommendation is to stop presenting this as a settled, unified finding. Also: generalized anxiety disorder is a documented exception, not significantly related to lower negative-emotion differentiation in at least one study — worth knowing before implying these deficits are universal across psychopathology.

**Alexithymia and emotion differentiation are related but not the same thing, and this lesson leans on both.** The alexithymia literature treats its construct as a relatively stable personality trait; the differentiation literature shows its construct moving day to day. Most of the large numbers above come from the former and most of the developmental arc comes from the latter. They agree in direction, which is the reason to trust the overall picture, but a reader should know they are not one measure.

**Naming a feeling does something measurable — but probably not what you would guess.** [Lieberman and colleagues, 2007](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) found that labeling an emotion reduced amygdala response and increased right ventrolateral prefrontal activity. [Burklund and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00221) extended this to labeling *one's own* feelings rather than an external image, in 39 adults aged 55–75, and found the amygdala reduction was statistically indistinguishable from that produced by cognitive reappraisal — psychology's most-studied regulation strategy. [Kircanski, Lieberman and Craske, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4721564/) took it out of the picture-viewing paradigm: 88 spider-fearful adults were randomized to label their fear, reappraise, be distracted, or just be exposed, and a week later, facing a different spider in a different room, the labeling group showed larger reductions in skin conductance than reappraisal (d=0.85), distraction (0.74), and exposure alone (0.64), with a dose-response — more fear words used, more fear reduction. **But the self-report picture is mixed and splits by paradigm.** Labeling reduced reported distress when people viewed unpleasant images in a lab (Burklund). It did not when people faced the thing they actually feared: no self-reported difference at any timepoint with a live spider (Kircanski), and in [Niles and colleagues, 2015](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/91f398qp), 100 public-speaking-anxious adults showed better physiological recovery from labeling but no self-report benefit — with heavier labelers reporting *worse* improvement. And labeling can interfere: two pre-registered experiments found that naming a feeling *before* reappraising made the reappraisal less effective, not more ([Ariely and colleagues, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13269579/), replicating Nook and colleagues, 2021). The consistent finding across all of it is physiological and neural; the inconsistent one is whether it makes you feel better.

**Can this be trained? Yes — with one result that deserves to be stated plainly.** [Mazza, Davis, Johnson, Larkin and Cole, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2026.121167) is a PRISMA-registered meta-analysis of 53 effect sizes from 47 studies, 3,368 people. Psychological intervention reduced alexithymia against control conditions at g=−0.52 [−0.71, −0.34]. Integrative interventions — combining psychoeducation, body-based work, a therapeutic relationship and group elements — produced the largest effects (−0.69), followed by CBT and third-wave approaches (−0.66) and arts therapies (−0.38). Three findings complicate that headline. **Publication bias was detected** (Egger's p=.006), with larger studies finding smaller effects, so −0.52 is likely an overestimate. **Meditation-based interventions were the weakest category tested** (g=−0.14, confidence interval crossing zero, from only 3 effect sizes), which the authors call unexpected and which conflicts with an earlier mindfulness-specific review that found a moderate effect. And in a partial analysis of the three facets, **interventions moved externally oriented thinking (−0.45) and difficulty describing feelings (−0.39) but not difficulty identifying feelings (−0.06, not significant)** — which is the facet that carries nearly every outcome reported in this section. That analysis rests on only 16 effect sizes with high heterogeneity, and the authors caution that firm conclusions can't be drawn from it, but it is the single most important open question about this lesson's premise and it should not be hidden.

**Almost everything here is self-report, and the construct is unawareness of one's own feelings.** That tension is worth sitting with. Nearly all of the studies above measured alexithymia with a questionnaire that asks people to report on the very capacity being tested. Researchers in the field have raised this directly, recommending multiple measures rather than the TAS-20 alone; almost no study does that.

**And the samples are narrow.** Of 59 intervention studies, only 14 reported participants' race or ethnicity, and of those, 73% of participants were White, European or European-American. No intervention study included autistic participants, despite a substantial literature arguing that emotional difficulties often attributed to autism are better explained by co-occurring alexithymia. Prevalence work outside Western, educated, industrialized countries is thin. Most of what this lesson can tell you comes from a narrow slice of the world.
