# Entries, in the order the reader meets them

## From The Challenge

### 1. Less aggression on angry days

**People who can tell their unpleasant feelings apart are less aggressive on the days they
are angriest.** Pond and colleagues 2012, *Emotion*. PDF read in full. Three daily-diary
studies of undergraduates, 628 total. Study 1: 199 people, 25 days — 17% fewer aggressive
tendencies on high-anger days among high differentiators. Study 2: 186 people, 21 days —
fewer provocation episodes reported, and 43% less aggression when provoked and angry.
Study 3: 243 people, 25 days — 16% less aggression when angry, partly explained by daily
emotional control. Differentiation measured by how much a person's ratings of different
negative emotions covary across days. Correlational; authors state the findings "may not
reveal causal relationships." Single-item daily measures throughout.

### 2. Loneliness carries the relationship damage

**The damage to a relationship runs through loneliness rather than directly.** Panahi and
colleagues 2018, *Journal of Family Medicine and Primary Care*. Full text read. 108 Iranian
couples — parents of children entering one primary school, convenience sample. Self-report
questionnaires: alexithymia, social support, loneliness, ENRICH marital scales. Path
analysis. The direct alexithymia-to-satisfaction path was non-significant and dropped from
the model; the surviving route was alexithymia to loneliness (0.34) to marital satisfaction
(−0.45), indirect effect −0.15. Cross-sectional, all self-report from the same person,
translated instruments, one region.

### 3. Self-harm

**People who struggle to identify their feelings harm themselves more.** Norman and
colleagues 2020, *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology*. Full text read including forest plots
and subgroup tables. Meta-analysis, 23 studies, 8,724 people, all TAS-20. Medium effect,
g=0.57. Driven by difficulty identifying feelings (0.61) and describing them (0.41);
externally-oriented thinking not significant. Adolescents larger than adults (0.69 vs 0.48).
Publication bias formally ruled out — symmetric funnel plot, trim-and-fill changed nothing.
Heterogeneity high (I²=70%). One longitudinal study inside the set found alexithymia
preceded self-harm by five months in 566 adolescents. Authors: "this cannot be interpreted
as causation."

### 4. Suicidal thinking, and why attempts differ

**They also think about suicide more — but the link to attempts is much weaker.** Hemming and
colleagues 2019, *Journal of Affective Disorders*. Full text read via PMC. 34 studies, 10,104
adults, clinical and general population, English-language only. Ideation r=0.54; behaviour
r=0.25. For ideation, externally-oriented thinking shows a real association (0.28) — the one
place in this literature where it does. Two quality flags found only in the full text: six
studies from a single Italian research group drove much of the ideation figure, and removing
them drops it from 0.54 to 0.41; and the authors' own multivariate check led them to conclude
"the relationship between suicide behaviours and alexithymia is not robust." I²=95% for
ideation.

### 5. Substance use

**And they drink and use more, most strongly where it matters clinically.** Honkalampi and
colleagues 2022, *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology*. Full text read via PMC. 52 studies
(50 cross-sectional, 2 longitudinal), 22,712 people, 2000–2019, all TAS-20. Overall d=0.62.
By population: clinical 0.83, general 0.51, students 0.28. Male-majority samples 0.85 against
0.44. Alcohol versus illicit drugs made no difference. No publication bias by Egger's test.
Authors rate overall study quality "poor or fair, mainly because of the cross-sectional
design" — with 2 of 52 longitudinal, direction is essentially untested.

### 6. Anger and the heart — two studies, one entry

**Anger you carry, and anger you let out, both show up in the heart.** Chida & Steptoe 2009,
*JACC*, and Mostofsky and colleagues 2013, *American Journal of Cardiology*. Both abstract
only. Chida & Steptoe: meta-analysis of prospective cohorts, 25 studies of initially healthy
people and 19 of people with existing heart disease; hazard ratio 1.19 for coronary events in
the healthy group; authors note indications of publication bias. Mostofsky: case-crossover,
3,886 people interviewed during hospitalisation for a heart attack, comparing anger outbursts
in the two hours before onset against each patient's own rate over the previous year;
incidence elevated 2.43-fold, rising with anger intensity. **Both measure anger, not awareness
of anger** — they illustrate the body carrying an unexamined feeling rather than testing
anything about noticing.

### 7. Physical symptoms — RESOLVED 20 Aug, PDF located in Downloads

**People who have the most trouble telling a feeling from a bodily sensation report the most
physical symptoms.** De Gucht & Heiser 2003, "Alexithymia and somatisation: a quantitative
review of the literature," *Journal of Psychosomatic Research* 54(5):425–434. Publisher
landing page with section snippets read; the full results tables are still behind the
paywall. Medline and PsycLIT searches, restricted to studies using one of the three versions
of the Toronto Alexithymia Scale. **16 publications yielding 18 study samples.**

Across all 18 samples, weighted by sample size, the **mean correlation between total
alexithymia and physical symptom reporting was 0.23** — small to moderate. All but two samples
found a significant positive association. Of the three components, **difficulty identifying
feelings showed the strongest association, and externally-oriented thinking was "virtually
unrelated."** That component is defined in this literature as difficulty identifying feelings
*and distinguishing between feelings and bodily sensations*, which is precisely the confusion
the lesson describes. People with a diagnosed somatoform condition scored higher than healthy
controls with moderate to large effects; comparisons against other medical and psychiatric
conditions were inconclusive.

**Two limits the authors state themselves.** Only one prospective study existed, so nothing
here establishes alexithymia as a predisposing factor rather than a correlate. And more
important for how the lesson words this: **the questionnaires used "can only check for
symptoms, not whether these symptoms are medically explained or not,"** so what was measured
is symptom reporting in general, not medically unexplained symptoms.

**CORRECTION OWED TO THE CHALLENGE.** The Challenge currently reads "the ones who report the
most *unexplained* physical complaints." The studies could not establish that the symptoms
were unexplained. The rest of that sentence — that the association is strongest in people who
struggle to tell an emotion from a sensation — is exactly right.

## From The Benefit

### 8. Losing social support over a year

**People who cannot identify their feelings lose social support over the following year.**
Ciarrochi, Heaven & Supavadeeprasit 2008, *Journal of Adolescence*. Abstract read verbatim.
667 Australian high school students measured in Grade 8 and again in Grade 9 — emotion
identification skill, social support, positive and negative affect. Structural equation
modelling. Low identification skill predicted increases in fear, decreases in positive affect,
and decreases in both the quality and the quantity of social support; among boys it also
predicted increases in sadness. Across the whole sample negative affect rose and positive
affect fell over the year regardless. **The only prospective study in the lesson.** Self-report
throughout, one country, one year.

### 9. Reading other people

**People who know their own feelings tend to read other people better.** Israelashvili, Oosterwijk,
Sauter & Fischer 2019, "Knowing me, knowing you: emotion differentiation in oneself is
associated with recognition of others' emotions," *Cognition and Emotion* 33(7):1461–1471.
Open access, primary text read in full.

Two studies. The first: 363 first-year psychology students, mean age 19. The second: 217 US
adults recruited online, mean age 37, **preregistered** and deliberately a different
population.

How your own feelings were measured: everyone rated the same 20 emotionally negative
photographs on the same 20 emotion words, 0 to 6. The score is how much a person's ratings
covary from picture to picture — rate every unpleasant image about the same way and you score
low, let the ratings move with what is in front of you and you score high. **Everyone worked
from an identical word list, so what the score captures is how discriminatingly the words were
used, not how many words anyone knew.**

How reading others was measured: three separate tasks — faces; faces with body and voice; and
eyes only. The association held on all three, standardised betas 0.235 to 0.322, all p<.001.

**The size is small.** The model explained between 4 and 10 percent of the variance in reading
other people. Their preregistered prediction — that knowing your own feelings would matter most
when the least information was available, the eyes-only task — failed; the three tasks did not
differ. Controlling for verbal ability, the association survived on two of the three.

**Direction is not established, and the authors say so first:** "experimental research will be
needed to illuminate whether emotion differentiation predicts emotion recognition or vice
versa." All the expressions people judged were posed by actors, and only negative emotions
were measured — the authors note there is no validated measure for the positive side.

### 10. The strategies work less well

**Low differentiation doesn't change which strategies people reach for — it makes the
strategies work less well.** Kalokerinos and colleagues 2019, *Psychological Science*. Abstract
read verbatim. Two experience-sampling studies: 200 people across 34,660 measurements, and 101
across 6,282. Verbatim: "few relationships between differentiation and the selection of
putatively adaptive or maladaptive strategies. Instead, we found interactions between
differentiation and strategies in predicting negative emotion. Among low differentiators, all
strategies (Study 1) and four of six strategies (Study 2) were more strongly associated with
increased negative emotion than they were among high differentiators." Authors' conclusion:
"effective regulation may underlie differentiation benefits." **The lesson's central claim,
stated by the researchers.** Abstract only — full text not read.

### 11. Telling unpleasant feelings apart and a range of harmful behaviors

**People who can tell whether they feel anxious, ashamed, or angry - instead of just "bad" -
binge eat less, act aggressively less, relapse on substances less, skip treatment less, and
self-injure less.** Seah & Coifman 2022, "Emotion Differentiation and Behavioral Dysregulation
in Clinical and Non-Clinical Samples: A Meta-Analysis," *Emotion*. PDF obtained, full text
read.

**17 samples, 2,182 people.** The paper pools binge eating, aggression, substance relapse,
treatment non-adherence, self-injury, and other behaviors into one measure. Against that
pooled measure, telling feelings apart correlates at r=-.15 [-.21, -.09], p<.001.

**The effect holds after accounting for how much negative emotion someone feels overall**
(partial r=-.09, p=.023, from the 11 studies with that data) - so it isn't just that
less-distressed people differentiate better. It also doesn't depend on clinical status
(Q=.01, p=.91).

**One piece of the pool looked different alone.** The single self-injury study inside it -
Zaki and colleagues 2013, 63 people with borderline personality disorder - was not
significant by itself: r=-.09, p=.59. The pooled result across all 17 samples is significant;
that one study, on its own, is not.

**Publication bias looks unlikely:** the funnel plot is symmetric, trim-and-fill barely moved
the estimate (to -.14), and the fail-safe N is 187 against 17 known studies. It's
correlational - the authors say directly that "more research is needed to establish
causality."

### 12. Awareness is the weakest predictor

**Of everything measured, noticing what you feel is the weakest predictor of how you behave.**
Smith and colleagues 2026, *Aggressive Behavior*. Open access, results section read directly.
137 articles, 171 studies, 918 effect sizes, 252,605 people. Multilevel models. Within the
subset using the standard emotion-regulation questionnaire — 425 effect sizes from 40 studies
— the six facets against aggression: impulse control 0.343, access to strategies 0.295,
goal-directed behaviour 0.240, non-acceptance 0.229, clarity 0.210, **emotional awareness
0.080**. Impulse control, access to strategies, non-acceptance and goal-directed were each
significantly stronger than awareness, all p<0.001. Awareness also had the lowest heterogeneity
(I²=30%), so the small effect is consistent rather than noisy. Authors' caveat: "the
over-reliance on cross-sectional studies poses difficulties for understanding directional
associations."

## From The Practice — currently invisible in the lesson

### 13. The check-in works

**Six weeks of rating named emotions several times a day improves the ability to tell
unpleasant feelings apart.** Widdershoven and colleagues 2019, *Journal of Affective
Disorders*. Full text read via author preprint. 79 outpatients with diagnosed major
depression, mean age 48, all medicated. 55 randomised to six weeks of rating ten named
emotions ten times a day, three days a week; 24 to control. Five-day assessment before and
after. Negative differentiation rose 0.17 to 0.37, d=0.43; controls didn't move. **The
intervention was the practice — no teaching, no vocabulary instruction, no therapy content**,
which makes it the cleanest attribution in the set. Positive differentiation trended up but
the study was underpowered to detect it (d=0.20, p=.136). No dose-response across 87–224
completed reports. The practice group also had weekly researcher meetings the controls did
not, though those meetings did not cover emotions.

### 14. Naming in the moment

**Naming fear out loud while you feel it leaves you calmer a week later, in a different
situation.** Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske 2012, *Psychological Science*. Full text read via
PMC. 88 spider-fearful adults, mean age 20.5, 82% female, randomised to four conditions
during live exposure to a real spider: describe your fear in emotion words, describe the
spider in neutral words, talk about your house, or exposure with no instruction. At one-week
follow-up **with a different spider in a different room**, the labelling group's skin
conductance dropped more than reappraisal (d=0.85), distraction (d=0.74) and exposure alone
(d=0.64). Dose-response within the labelling group: more fear words, larger drop (r=−.288).
Approach behaviour same direction, marginal. **"No significant between-group differences
emerged at any timepoint" for self-reported fear.** Authors' limits: the reappraisal
instruction was thin next to real therapy, the no-instruction group may have labelled
silently anyway, experimenters weren't blind.

### 16. The body scan

**Three months of body scan improves your ability to read the body for what you're feeling —
but not your ability to detect sensation.** Bornemann and colleagues 2014, *Frontiers in
Psychology*. Full text read. 148 people in the ReSource training against 80 retest controls.
20 minutes of body scan plus 10 minutes of breath meditation, five days a week for 13 weeks,
plus weekly two-hour classes and a three-day opening retreat; mean total home practice 36.5
hours. Measured on the eight MAIA subscales. Self-regulation via body attention d=0.72;
attention regulation 0.54; body listening 0.40; trusting the body 0.29; **the
sensation-to-emotion link 0.17, weakest of the five that moved; raw noticing of sensation did
not move at all.** Liking the practice predicted change on six of eight scales and integrating
it into daily life on seven of eight, rs 0.19–0.43; practice duration predicted only two,
weakly. Screened healthy adults, mean age 40s, 70–78% female, German, highly educated.

### 17. The partner check-in — COI + DESIGN FLAG

**Twelve minutes a day with a partner improves putting feelings into words, more than
practising alone does.** Silveira, Godara, Faschinger & Singer 2023, *Journal of Affective
Disorders* 341:162–169. Primary text read for abstract, introduction and the alexithymia
results; methods and full results paywalled. 285 subclinical adults. **Not a two-arm trial** —
there is a waitlist control and a fourth condition whose definition isn't visible in the
accessible text, and all reported contrasts are against waitlist, so "the dyad beat solo
practice" is an inference from two separate comparisons rather than a head-to-head test.
Against waitlist: total alexithymia d=−0.39 for the dyad and −0.29 for solo mindfulness;
difficulty describing feelings −0.50 and −0.37. Dropout 25% in the dyad against 15% solo.
The authors abstained from objective measures entirely — every interoception outcome is
self-report or app-based diary. **Declared conflict of interest: Tania Singer was scientific
and curriculum advisor 2021–2023 to a start-up commercialising the Affect Dyad.**

### 18. Bodily locations of emotion — DRAFTED, decide whether it earns its place

**Where people feel an emotion in the body is consistent enough that a computer can tell
which emotion it is, and it holds across cultures.** Nummenmaa and colleagues 2014, *PNAS*.
PDF read in full. Five experiments, 701 participants. People were shown a blank body outline
alongside an emotion word, a story, a film clip or a face, and coloured in where they felt
activity increasing and where they felt it decreasing. The maps came out distinct enough per
emotion that a classifier could identify the emotion from the colouring well above chance,
and they matched across Finnish, Swedish and Taiwanese participants — a correlation of about
0.70 between the West European and East Asian groups.

**What it supports:** that "where do you feel it" is a real question with a stable answer,
rather than a prompt people answer at random. That is the assumption the locate-it step in
the check-in rests on, and nothing else in the lesson supports it.

**What it does not support:** that finding the location does anything for you. Nobody was
trained, nothing was measured before and after, and no outcome was tracked. **The authors
also state they cannot rule out that the maps reflect learned language about emotion rather
than actual bodily patterning** — people may be colouring in what we all say about anger
rather than what anger does. The task is self-report throughout.

**Decision, 20 Aug: keep.** It justifies asking the question, which is the job it needs to
do — the check-in asks people to locate the feeling and nothing else in the lesson supports
that step. It says nothing about the benefit of asking, and should not be written as if it
does.

### 19. More is not better for everyone

**Turning attention up is not good for everyone.** Goldman and colleagues 2025, *Journal of
Health Psychology*. Abstract read verbatim. 460 women with breast cancer, followed one year.
Higher emotional awareness combined with low social support predicted **greater** depression
and lower optimism. Higher awareness predicted greater depression at baseline but lower
depression at nine-month follow-up. Authors read it as awareness increasing sensitivity to
whatever support is available. One clinical population, one illness.

### 20. Hard days run backwards

**On the days you most want this, you will have the least of it.** Erbas and colleagues 2018,
*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. PDF supplied, full text read. 200 first-year
students, three measurement bursts across a year, 190 and 177 retained at waves two and three.
Stress on one day predicted lower negative-emotion differentiation the next day (p=.010),
holding after controlling for that day's own stress. The obvious confound was ruled out:
anger, anxiety, sadness, depression and loneliness all correlated with differentiation on the
same day but **none predicted it forward the way stress did**. The authors resist calling it
dysfunction — no wellbeing measure moderated the effect, and they argue the narrowing may be
adaptive.

## Uncertainties and opposing views

### U1. Four constructs that disagree

**"Emotional awareness" is at least four different things that don't agree with each other.**
Maroti, Lilliengren & Bileviciute-Ljungar 2018, *Frontiers in Psychology*. Full text fetched
and summarised — **get the PDF before printing figures**. 21 studies, 28 independent samples,
2,857 people. The self-report alexithymia questionnaire and the performance-based awareness
scale correlate at r=−0.122. No moderation by sample type, age or gender. The detail that
matters most: the self-report measure correlates with negative affect and the performance
measure does not, and **controlling for negative affect made the self-report associations
non-significant while leaving the performance ones intact.** Authors: the two "measure
different aspects of emotional functioning," and should never be used interchangeably.

### U2. Almost all correlational

Two of the three exceptions run in the direction the lesson assumes — Ciarrochi's year, and
the five-month study inside Norman's meta-analysis.

### U3. Identifying may be the facet that trains least

One meta-analysis of 47 controlled studies found interventions moved *describing* feelings and
externally-oriented thinking but left *identifying* them essentially unchanged; another study
of 170 adults found the exact reverse. Genuinely contested, and the structural risk to the
lesson.

### U4. Everything measures the skill, not life

Every practice here was measured on the skill itself — questionnaires, lab tasks,
physiological readings — with Kircanski the single exception. **None measured whether life got
better.**

### U5. No adolescent evidence for any practice

The trials screened out clinical cases; mean ages run 40 to 48, mostly female, German or
American, highly educated.
