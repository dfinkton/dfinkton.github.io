# Entries, in the order the reader meets them

## From The Challenge

### 1. People who can tell their unpleasant feelings apart are less aggressive on the days they are angriest.

Pond and colleagues 2012, *Emotion*. PDF read in full. Three daily-diary studies of
undergraduates, 628 total. Study 1: 199 people, 25 days — 17% fewer aggressive tendencies on
high-anger days among high differentiators. Study 2: 186 people, 21 days — fewer provocation
episodes reported, and 43% less aggression when provoked and angry. Study 3: 243 people, 25
days — 16% less aggression when angry, partly explained by daily emotional control.
Differentiation measured by how much a person's ratings of different negative emotions
covary across days. Correlational; authors state the findings "may not reveal causal
relationships." Single-item daily measures throughout.

### 2. The damage to a relationship runs through loneliness rather than directly.

Panahi and colleagues 2018, *Journal of Family Medicine and Primary Care*. Full text read.
108 Iranian couples — parents of children entering one primary school, convenience sample.
Self-report questionnaires: alexithymia, social support, loneliness, ENRICH marital scales.
Path analysis. The direct alexithymia-to-satisfaction path was non-significant and dropped
from the model; the surviving route was alexithymia to loneliness (0.34) to marital
satisfaction (−0.45), indirect effect −0.15. Cross-sectional, all self-report from the same
person, translated instruments, one region.

### 3. People who struggle to identify their feelings harm themselves more.

Norman and colleagues 2020, *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology*. Full text read including
forest plots and subgroup tables. Meta-analysis, 23 studies, 8,724 people, all TAS-20.
Medium effect, g=0.57. Driven by difficulty identifying feelings (0.61) and describing them
(0.41); externally-oriented thinking not significant. Adolescents larger than adults (0.69
vs 0.48). Publication bias formally ruled out — symmetric funnel plot, trim-and-fill changed
nothing. Heterogeneity high (I²=70%). One longitudinal study inside the set found alexithymia
preceded self-harm by five months in 566 adolescents. Authors: "this cannot be interpreted
as causation."

### 4. They also think about suicide more — but the link to attempts is much weaker.

Hemming and colleagues 2019, *Journal of Affective Disorders*. Full text read via PMC. 34
studies, 10,104 adults, clinical and general population, English-language only. Ideation
r=0.54; behaviour r=0.25. For ideation, externally-oriented thinking shows a real association
(0.28) — the one place in this literature where it does. Two quality flags found only in the
full text: six studies from a single Italian research group drove much of the ideation
figure, and removing them drops it from 0.54 to 0.41; and the authors' own multivariate check
led them to conclude "the relationship between suicide behaviours and alexithymia is not
robust." I²=95% for ideation.

### 5. They also drink and use more, most strongly where it matters clinically.

Honkalampi and colleagues 2022, *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology*. Full text read via
PMC. 52 studies (50 cross-sectional, 2 longitudinal), 22,712 people, 2000–2019, all TAS-20.
Overall d=0.62. By population: clinical 0.83, general 0.51, students 0.28. Male-majority
samples 0.85 against 0.44. Alcohol versus illicit drugs made no difference. No publication
bias by Egger's test. Authors rate overall study quality "poor or fair, mainly because of
the cross-sectional design" — with 2 of 52 longitudinal, direction is essentially untested.

### 6. Anger you carry, and anger you let out, both show up in the heart.

Chida & Steptoe 2009, *JACC*, and Mostofsky and colleagues 2013, *American Journal of
Cardiology* — two studies pooled into one entry. Both abstract only. Chida & Steptoe:
meta-analysis of prospective cohorts, 25 studies of initially healthy people and 19 of
people with existing heart disease; hazard ratio 1.19 for coronary events in the healthy
group; authors note indications of publication bias. Mostofsky: case-crossover, 3,886 people
interviewed during hospitalisation for a heart attack, comparing anger outbursts in the two
hours before onset against each patient's own rate over the previous year; incidence
elevated 2.43-fold, rising with anger intensity. Both measure anger, not awareness of anger
— they illustrate the body carrying an unexamined feeling rather than testing anything about
noticing.

### 7. People who have the most trouble telling a feeling from a bodily sensation report the most physical symptoms.

De Gucht & Heiser 2003, "Alexithymia and somatisation: a quantitative review of the
literature," *Journal of Psychosomatic Research* 54(5):425–434. Publisher landing page with
section snippets read; the full results tables are still behind the paywall. Medline and
PsycLIT searches, restricted to studies using one of the three versions of the Toronto
Alexithymia Scale. 16 publications yielding 18 study samples. Across all 18 samples, weighted
by sample size, the mean correlation between total alexithymia and physical symptom
reporting was **0.23** — small to moderate. All but two samples found a significant positive
association. Of the three components, difficulty identifying feelings showed the strongest
association, and externally-oriented thinking was "virtually unrelated." That component is
defined in this literature as difficulty identifying feelings and distinguishing between
feelings and bodily sensations, which is precisely the confusion the lesson describes.
People with a diagnosed somatoform condition scored higher than healthy controls with
moderate to large effects; comparisons against other medical and psychiatric conditions were
inconclusive. Only one prospective study existed, so nothing here establishes alexithymia as
a predisposing factor rather than a correlate. And the questionnaires used "can only check
for symptoms, not whether these symptoms are medically explained or not," so what was
measured is symptom reporting in general, not medically unexplained symptoms.

*(Correction owed to The Challenge: it currently says "unexplained" physical complaints,
which this study can't establish.)*

## From The Benefit

### 8. People who cannot identify their feelings lose social support over the following year.

Ciarrochi, Heaven & Supavadeeprasit 2008, *Journal of Adolescence*. Abstract read verbatim.
667 Australian high school students measured in Grade 8 and again in Grade 9 — emotion
identification skill, social support, positive and negative affect. Structural equation
modelling. Low identification skill predicted increases in fear, decreases in positive
affect, and decreases in both the quality and the quantity of social support; among boys it
also predicted increases in sadness. Across the whole sample negative affect rose and
positive affect fell over the year regardless. The only prospective study in the lesson.
Self-report throughout, one country, one year.

### 9. People who know their own feelings tend to read other people better.

Israelashvili, Oosterwijk, Sauter & Fischer 2019, "Knowing me, knowing you: emotion
differentiation in oneself is associated with recognition of others' emotions," *Cognition
and Emotion* 33(7):1461–1471. Open access, primary text read in full. Two studies. The
first: 363 first-year psychology students, mean age 19. The second: 217 US adults recruited
online, mean age 37, preregistered and deliberately a different population. Your own
feelings were measured by rating the same 20 emotionally negative photographs on the same 20
emotion words, 0 to 6 — the score is how much a person's ratings covary from picture to
picture, so **everyone worked from an identical word list, and what the score captures is how
discriminatingly the words were used, not how many words anyone knew.** Reading others was
measured with three separate tasks — faces; faces with body and voice; and eyes only — and
the association held on all three, betas 0.235 to 0.322, all p<.001. The model explained
between 4 and 10 percent of the variance. Their preregistered prediction — that this would
matter most when the least information was available, the eyes-only task — failed; the three
tasks did not differ. Controlling for verbal ability, the association survived on two of the
three. Direction is not established, and the authors say so first: "experimental research
will be needed to illuminate whether emotion differentiation predicts emotion recognition or
vice versa." All expressions were posed by actors, and only negative emotions were measured.

### 10. Low differentiation doesn't change which strategies people reach for — it makes the strategies work less well.

Kalokerinos and colleagues 2019, *Psychological Science*. Abstract read verbatim. Two
experience-sampling studies: 200 people across 34,660 measurements, and 101 across 6,282.
Verbatim: "few relationships between differentiation and the selection of putatively
adaptive or maladaptive strategies. Instead, we found interactions between differentiation
and strategies in predicting negative emotion. Among low differentiators, all strategies
(Study 1) and four of six strategies (Study 2) were more strongly associated with increased
negative emotion than they were among high differentiators." Authors' conclusion: "effective
regulation may underlie differentiation benefits." Abstract only — full text not read.

### 11. People who can tell whether they feel anxious, ashamed, or angry — instead of just "bad" — are less likely to act aggressively, binge eat, relapse into substance use, skip treatment, or self-injure.

Seah & Coifman 2022, *Emotion*. PDF obtained, full text read. Meta-analysis, 17 samples,
2,182 people. The paper pools those behaviors into one measure, and against that pooled
measure telling feelings apart correlates at r=−.15, p<.001. The effect holds after
accounting for how much negative emotion someone feels overall, so it isn't just that
less-distressed people differentiate better, and it doesn't depend on clinical status. The
single self-injury study inside the pool — 63 people with borderline personality disorder —
was not significant on its own (r=−.09, p=.59); the pooled result across all 17 samples is.
Publication bias looks unlikely: the funnel plot is symmetric and the fail-safe N is 187
against 17 known studies. Entirely correlational — the authors say directly that "more
research is needed to establish causality."

### 12. Of everything measured, noticing what you feel is the weakest predictor of how you behave.

Smith and colleagues 2026, *Aggressive Behavior*. Open access, results section read
directly. 137 articles, 171 studies, 918 effect sizes, 252,605 people. Multilevel models.
Within the subset using the standard emotion-regulation questionnaire — 425 effect sizes
from 40 studies — the six facets against aggression: impulse control 0.343, access to
strategies 0.295, goal-directed behaviour 0.240, non-acceptance 0.229, clarity 0.210,
emotional awareness 0.080. Impulse control, access to strategies, non-acceptance and
goal-directed were each significantly stronger than awareness, all p<0.001. Awareness also
had the lowest heterogeneity (I²=30%), so the small effect is consistent rather than noisy.
Authors' caveat: "the over-reliance on cross-sectional studies poses difficulties for
understanding directional associations."

## From The Practice — currently invisible in the lesson

### 13. Six weeks of rating named emotions several times a day improves the ability to tell unpleasant feelings apart.

Widdershoven and colleagues 2019, *Journal of Affective Disorders*. Full text read via
author preprint. 79 outpatients with diagnosed major depression, mean age 48, all medicated.
55 randomised to six weeks of rating ten named emotions ten times a day, three days a week;
24 to control. Five-day assessment before and after. Negative differentiation rose 0.17 to
0.37, d=0.43; controls didn't move. The intervention was the practice — no teaching, no
vocabulary instruction, no therapy content — which makes it the cleanest attribution in the
set. Positive differentiation trended up but the study was underpowered to detect it
(d=0.20, p=.136). No dose-response across 87–224 completed reports. The practice group also
had weekly researcher meetings the controls did not, though those meetings did not cover
emotions.

### 14. Naming fear out loud while you feel it leaves you calmer a week later, in a different situation.

Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske 2012, *Psychological Science*. Full text read via PMC. 88
spider-fearful adults, mean age 20.5, 82% female, randomised to four conditions during live
exposure to a real spider: describe your fear in emotion words, describe the spider in
neutral words, talk about your house, or exposure with no instruction. At one-week follow-up
with a different spider in a different room, the labelling group's skin conductance dropped
more than reappraisal (d=0.85), distraction (d=0.74) and exposure alone (d=0.64).
Dose-response within the labelling group: more fear words, larger drop (r=−.288). Approach
behaviour same direction, marginal. "No significant between-group differences emerged at any
timepoint" for self-reported fear. Authors' limits: the reappraisal instruction was thin next
to real therapy, the no-instruction group may have labelled silently anyway, experimenters
weren't blind.

### 16. Three months of body scan improves your ability to read the body for what you're feeling — but not your ability to detect sensation.

Bornemann and colleagues 2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*. Full text read. 148 people in the
ReSource training against 80 retest controls. 20 minutes of body scan plus 10 minutes of
breath meditation, five days a week for 13 weeks, plus weekly two-hour classes and a
three-day opening retreat; mean total home practice 36.5 hours. Measured on the eight MAIA
subscales. Self-regulation via body attention d=0.72; attention regulation 0.54; body
listening 0.40; trusting the body 0.29; the sensation-to-emotion link 0.17, weakest of the
five that moved; raw noticing of sensation did not move at all. Liking the practice
predicted change on six of eight scales and integrating it into daily life on seven of
eight; practice duration predicted only two, weakly. Screened healthy adults, mean age 40s,
70–78% female, German, highly educated.

### 17. Twelve minutes a day with a partner improves putting feelings into words, more than practising alone does.

Silveira, Godara, Faschinger & Singer 2023, *Journal of Affective Disorders* 341:162–169.
Primary text read for abstract, introduction and the alexithymia results; methods and full
results paywalled. 285 subclinical adults. Not a two-arm trial — there is a waitlist control
and a fourth condition whose definition isn't visible in the accessible text, and all
reported contrasts are against waitlist, so "the dyad beat solo practice" is an inference
from two separate comparisons rather than a head-to-head test. Against waitlist: total
alexithymia d=−0.39 for the dyad and −0.29 for solo mindfulness; difficulty describing
feelings −0.50 and −0.37. Dropout 25% in the dyad against 15% solo. The authors abstained
from objective measures entirely — every interoception outcome is self-report or app-based
diary. Declared conflict of interest: Tania Singer was scientific and curriculum advisor
2021–2023 to a start-up commercialising the Affect Dyad.

### 18. Where people feel an emotion in the body is consistent enough that a computer can tell which emotion it is, and it holds across cultures.

Nummenmaa and colleagues 2014, *PNAS*. PDF read in full. Five experiments, 701 participants.
People were shown a blank body outline alongside an emotion word, a story, a film clip or a
face, and coloured in where they felt activity increasing and where they felt it decreasing.
The maps came out distinct enough per emotion that a classifier could identify the emotion
from the colouring well above chance, and they matched across Finnish, Swedish and Taiwanese
participants — a correlation of about 0.70 between the West European and East Asian groups.
This justifies asking "where do you feel it" as a real question with a stable answer, which
is the assumption the locate-it step in the check-in rests on. It does not show that finding
the location does anything for you — nobody was trained, nothing was measured before and
after. The authors also state they cannot rule out that the maps reflect learned language
about emotion rather than actual bodily patterning. The task is self-report throughout.

### 19. Turning attention up is not good for everyone.

Goldman and colleagues 2025, *Journal of Health Psychology*. Abstract read verbatim. 460
women with breast cancer, followed one year. Higher emotional awareness combined with low
social support predicted greater depression and lower optimism. Higher awareness predicted
greater depression at baseline but lower depression at nine-month follow-up. Authors read it
as awareness increasing sensitivity to whatever support is available. One clinical
population, one illness.

### 20. On the days you most want this, you will have the least of it.

Erbas and colleagues 2018, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. PDF supplied,
full text read. 200 first-year students, three measurement bursts across a year, 190 and 177
retained at waves two and three. Stress on one day predicted lower negative-emotion
differentiation the next day (p=.010), holding after controlling for that day's own stress.
The obvious confound was ruled out: anger, anxiety, sadness, depression and loneliness all
correlated with differentiation on the same day but none predicted it forward the way stress
did. The authors resist calling it dysfunction — no wellbeing measure moderated the effect,
and they argue the narrowing may be adaptive.

## Uncertainties and opposing views

### U1. "Emotional awareness" is at least four different things that don't agree with each other.

Maroti, Lilliengren & Bileviciute-Ljungar 2018, *Frontiers in Psychology*. Full text fetched
and summarised — get the PDF before printing figures. 21 studies, 28 independent samples,
2,857 people. The self-report alexithymia questionnaire and the performance-based awareness
scale correlate at r=−0.122. No moderation by sample type, age or gender. The self-report
measure correlates with negative affect and the performance measure does not, and
controlling for negative affect made the self-report associations non-significant while
leaving the performance ones intact. Authors: the two "measure different aspects of
emotional functioning," and should never be used interchangeably.

### U2. Almost everything in this lesson is correlational.

Two exceptions run forward in time and both point the direction the lesson assumes —
Ciarrochi's one-year study, and the five-month study inside Norman's meta-analysis.

### U3. One meta-analysis found interventions move describing feelings and externally-oriented thinking but not identifying them — another found the opposite.

47 controlled studies against a study of 170 adults. Genuinely contested, and it's the
structural risk to the lesson, since identifying is the skill this lesson teaches.
