## What it's for
Everyone argues. The difference is whether you can stop mid-argument without finishing it, and get back to care. This is distinct from Repair, which is what you say afterward, and from De-escalation, which is helping someone else come down.

## The core finding
Gottman's repair-attempt work: repair attempts are **universal** — both stable and unstable couples make them. What separates the groups is whether they are **accepted**. In stable couples, attempts were met with softening, acknowledgment, an "I know." In divorcing couples they were ignored, dismissed, or answered with counter-attack. **Acceptance, not frequency, tracks with outcome.** And rejected repairs appear to do more harm than no repair at all — you learn not to reach.

His definition is deliberately broad: any statement or action, silly or otherwise, that prevents negativity from escalating out of control. Humor, a request to slow down, touch, bringing the other person a cup of coffee mid-argument.

## Practices
- Building a personal repertoire. Gottman's six categories, derived from asking thousands of couples which phrases would actually calm them: *I feel* / *I'm sorry* / *Get to yes* / *I need to calm down* / *Stop action* / *I appreciate*.
- **Timing.** His coding work found evidence for pre-emptive repair in the first three minutes, and that these were the most effective. The common failure is attempting repair after someone is already flooded.
- **Accepting one.** The harder half. It requires dropping being right while still activated, and it is the half the research says predicts outcome.
- **Practicing in low-stakes friction** so the move is available in high-stakes conflict — the graduated-difficulty principle from lesson 30.
- Taking a break, and returning. A break that does not end in return is stonewalling.

## Studies
- Gottman, J. M., et al. Development of the Repair Attempts Scale — observational coding of how newlywed couples naturally attempt repair during conflict; effectiveness criteria were reduced negative affect or increased positive affect; analyzed across the interaction and in five 3-minute blocks.
- Gottman, J. M., et al. The 130-newlywed-couples study testing seven process models: anger as a dangerous emotion, active listening, negative affect reciprocity, harsh startup by the wife, **de-escalation**, positive affect models, and **physiological soothing of the male**.
- Levenson, R. W., & Gottman, J. M. (1983). *JPSP, 45*, 587–597. — Physiological linkage accounted for 60% of variance in marital satisfaction; distressed couples showed greater linkage, more negative affect reciprocity, sharper during conflict. **Context, not the practice.**
- Levenson, R. W., & Gottman, J. M. (1985). *JPSP, 49*, 85–94. — Higher arousal in 1980 predicted decline in satisfaction by 1983.
- Levenson, Carstensen & Gottman — 151 long-term couples; older couples showed more affective positivity and lower arousal; husbands felt more negative the more aroused, wives' affect and arousal uncorrelated.
- Bloch, Haase & Levenson — wives' downregulation of negative emotion during conflict predicted satisfaction, mediated by constructive communication.
- Schreiber et al. (2020) — disrupted couple coregulation predicted decreased satisfaction one year later.

## Honest notes — required
The physiological studies used small samples (n = 30) with correlations reported in the .70s–.90s across multiple channels, which is the configuration where overfitting produces spectacular numbers. Gottman's divorce-prediction accuracy claims have been methodologically criticized for post-hoc model fitting. **The phenomenon and the practice are well described; the specific numbers are not reliable.** Say so, and build on the behavior rather than the statistics. Much of the accessible writing on repair attempts is practitioner and commercial material rather than primary research — cite the coding studies, not the checklists.
