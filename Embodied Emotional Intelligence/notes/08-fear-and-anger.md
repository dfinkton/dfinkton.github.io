## Practices
Graduated exposure starting at a 3. Substitution — anger's instruction is *defend the boundary*; escalation is one vehicle among several.

## One thing to make explicit: avoidance hides inside apparent approach
You can go to the party and spend it on your phone. You can have the hard conversation while keeping it abstract enough that nothing lands. The rep only counts if you let the thing reach you — the same concrete-versus-abstract distinction that runs through lessons 6 and 9.

## Studies
Exposure and habituation are the strongest mechanistic evidence in clinical psychology. PTSD meta-analyses: exposure-based treatments keep improving after treatment ends (d ≈ 0.27).

## Venting — researched 2026-08-13, relocated from the Lesson 2 register

**Bushman, B. J. (2002). "Does Venting Anger Feed or Extinguish the Flame? Catharsis, Rumination,
Distraction, Anger, and Aggressive Responding." *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*
28(6):724–731.** Primary text read.

Hitting a punching bag while thinking about the person who provoked you did **not** reduce anger or
aggression. But two corrections to how this is usually cited, both of them errors I made first:

- **The "154-study review" often attributed to Bushman is not in this paper.** He cites
  **Geen & Quanty (1977)**. Do not repeat the attribution.
- **"Doing nothing beat venting" is overstated** — the distraction-vs-control contrast was
  **non-significant on both measures.**

**Geen & Quanty's moderators are the part that matters for this lesson.** Venting reduced arousal
**only when the anger was expressed directly at the provocateur, and only when retaliation was not
expected.** Those two conditions are otherwise arbitrary — but they are exactly what Darryl's frame
predicts: *the boundary got set, and it was safe to set it.* A pillow sets no boundary with anyone;
neither does a sympathetic friend. **Recorded as a prediction the action-tendency frame makes, not
as a tested moderator.** Nothing has been designed to check it. See also lesson 11.

**Nils & Rimé (2012) cannot test it** — their stimulus was a film about atrocities committed by
strangers, so there was no boundary available to set and no action tendency that could complete.
Full entry in `source-ledger.md`; the lesson-25 relevance is the social-sharing result, not the
anger one.

## Anger is missing from the literature that would justify if-then planning

The strongest technique in the emotion-regulation literature is if-then planning (see lesson 19).
**It has never been tested on anger.** Every result is disgust, spider fear, button-pressing,
cigarette refusal, flossing, homework.

- **Schweiger Gallo et al. (2009)**, JPSP: "It should also be possible to ad hoc regulate other
  important daily emotions, **such as anger**" — a discussion sentence, not a result. Their three
  experiments used disgust and spider fear, both stimulus-driven withdrawal emotions with **no
  action tendency toward another person.**
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006)** carry two anger examples, both hypothetical: Appendix I's *"if I
  feel my anger rise, then I will tell myself to stay calm and not aggress back"* is an
  illustration of the format; the Future Directions passage proposes a therapist-perspective plan
  for "an obnoxious person" and flags it as new work.
- **Webb, Miles & Sheeran (2012)**, k=306: anger **did not moderate** regulation success
  (β = −0.137, ns) — their text says anger, anxiety and disgust "did not influence effect sizes."
  Correcting an earlier note here that said anger was independently hardest to regulate.
- **Boustani et al.**, 58 prevention programs: **anger management appears in 12%.** Assertiveness
  training appears in 45% — a different thing, and worth keeping distinct.
- **Chorpita**, 322 RCTs: for childhood anger, the winning protocols are **caregiver contingency
  management**; insight building scored .09.

**Chervonsky & Hunt's "anger exception" is not usable as stated.** The STAXI defines Anger-Out as
*verbally or physically aggressive* behaviour and files assertive expression separately under Anger
Control-Out. So the finding may only mean "aggression hurts social outcomes." **Get the
operationalisation before citing the effect size.** How anger is voiced is very likely the whole
question, and these studies do not tell us.
