## Key points
Organized by what each does to the machinery, not by legal status. SSRIs, alcohol, caffeine, cannabis, MDMA, opiates.

## SSRIs and emotional blunting — researched, and the numbers are large
- Goodwin, Price, De Bodinat & Laredo (2017) — 669 MDD patients on antidepressant monotherapy plus 150 drug-free previously depressed controls; overall emotional blunting prevalence **46%** (men 52%, women 44%).
- Systematic review, *Acta Neuropsychiatrica* (2023), 50 articles — antidepressant-induced apathy prevalence 5.8–50% overall, **20–92% for SSRIs specifically.** Emerges independently of diagnosis, age, and treatment outcome. **Dose-dependent and reversible.**
- Padala et al. (2020) — chart review, 125 SSRI outpatients, up to 92% showed clinically significant apathy.
- Read & Williams (2018) — 71% of antidepressant users reported emotional numbing, 66% "not feeling like myself," 60% reduced positive feelings.
- New Zealand survey, >1,800 patients — 60% reported feeling emotionally numb.
- Bolling & Kohlenberg (2004), 161 patients — ~20% "inability to cry," 46% "narrowed range of affect."
- Fava et al. (2006), 117 MDD patients — ~30% on SSRI reported some apathy.
- Emotional blunting is a common reason for discontinuation. Current clinical guidance recommends routine screening in all patients on SSRIs/SNRIs, using tools such as the Oxford Depression Questionnaire. Management is dose reduction first-line, or switching to lower-risk agents.
- Patients describe it as qualitatively different from residual depression — emerging or worsening after treatment begins. Some describe emotions becoming "more cognitive," more like thoughts than feelings.

## Required framing for this essay
This is not an argument against antidepressants. They reduce genuine suffering, and the effect described is dose-dependent and reversible. But a series built on reading emotional signal has an obligation to say plainly that a widely prescribed medication reduces signal amplitude in a large fraction of people taking it, and that this is screenable and adjustable. **The essay must direct readers to their prescriber and must not encourage anyone to stop or change a dose on their own.** That guardrail is not optional.

## Caffeine — the cost is upstream of where people look for it
- Klevebrant, L., & Frick, A. (2022). Effects of caffeine on anxiety and panic attacks in patients with panic disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *General Hospital Psychiatry, 74*, 22–31. Doses above roughly 400–750 mg — four to five cups of coffee — induced panic attacks in about **half of individuals with panic disorder** and **2% of healthy controls**, and raised subjective anxiety. Below about 300 mg, effects on alertness and mood in healthy adults are generally positive.
- Hoppe et al. (2025), randomised placebo-controlled crossover, 150 mg. At a *low* dose, caffeine produced no differential anxiogenic effect at rest — but in **both** panic-disorder and healthy participants it increased skin conductance responses to neutral and emotional faces, augmented costly avoidance behaviour, and impaired exteroceptive attention. **A dose that does not make you feel anxious still raises physiological reactivity, increases avoidance, and pulls attention off the outside world.**
- Sleep. Gardiner, C., et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Sleep Medicine Reviews, 69*, 101764. Evening caffeine suppresses melatonin and delays circadian onset; adult effects on slow-wave sleep are large.

## The framing
Caffeine's main cost is not jitteriness. It is sleep — which is essay 31, the largest single lever in the series. Caffeine sits upstream of the thing that matters most, and the loop closes: worse sleep, more caffeine.

## Cannabis — used to regulate, associated with worse regulation, and specifically in the acceptance half
- Systematic review of emotion regulation and cannabis use in young people (*Frontiers in Adolescent Medicine*, 2026): use in adolescence and young adulthood is associated with **specific rather than global** emotion-regulation difficulties — concentrated in impulse control, goal-directed behaviour, and **non-acceptance of emotional responses**, measured with the DERS and ERQ. Preliminary; few eligible studies; causal direction unestablished.

That non-acceptance finding is worth pausing on. It is precisely the capacity that essay 3 identifies as the thing that converts noticing into relief rather than into more reactivity.

- Heitzeg, M. M., Cope, L. M., Martz, M. E., Hardee, J. E., & Zucker, R. A. (2015). *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, 16*, 71–83. Longitudinal, ages 13 to 23. Cannabis users reported consistently greater negative emotionality than controls — and **while controls' negative emotionality declined with age, users' stayed elevated.** Caudal dorsolateral prefrontal activation mediated the association. **The developmental improvement curve that essay 42 is built on did not happen for them.**
- Heavy users showed *less* neural activation to negative words across temporal, prefrontal and occipital cortices, insula and amygdala — while reporting *more* negative emotionality. A dissociation between felt negativity and neural response, which is blunting.
- Risk context: around one in five users develop cannabis use disorder; high-potency cannabis has been estimated to account for 12% of first-episode psychosis.

## A counterpoint that keeps the essay honest
The standard story is that coping motives reflect emotion-regulation deficits. A 2025 study found coping motives for alcohol and cannabis better reflect **negative emotionality** than emotion-regulation deficits in young adults — undermining the claim that using-to-cope indicates missing skills rather than a broader tendency toward negative affect.

## Opioids — the clearest case of a drug that works by turning the signal down
- Acute administration blunts emotional reactivity to stimuli. A review of pharmacological MRI studies found that alcohol, analgesics and psychedelics all reduce neural reactivity to negative emotional material; stimulants including caffeine *increase* activation during emotional viewing; cannabinoid effects are mixed.
- Chronic use: the allostatic model describes **hyperkatifeia** — a constellation of hyperalgesia, dysphoria, anhedonia and distress arising from prolonged opioid exposure in the context of chronic pain.
- People with opioid use disorder show lower neural, electrophysiological and heart-rate-variability responses to **natural, non-drug** reward cues than controls — and blunted response has been associated with future use. The world gets quieter, which makes the drug the only loud thing left.
- The loop has a name in this literature: using to self-medicate negative affect appears to blunt regulatory capacity, which worsens hyperkatifeia, which drives craving and further use.

## The caveats are substantial and belong in the essay
Anhedonia is often but **not always** observed in opioid-using populations, and changes in reward sensitivity after chronic use may be explained by co-occurring vulnerabilities rather than by opioid exposure itself.

## One hopeful finding that connects across the series
Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement has been found to remediate anhedonia in chronic opioid use by enhancing neurophysiological responses during **savouring of natural rewards** — that is, a positive-attention practice, of the kind essays 4 and 30 describe.

## MDMA — status as of the last check, and it will have moved
FDA issued a Complete Response Letter rejecting MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD in August 2024, after an advisory committee voted 10–1 against. **But the rejection was contested inside the field.** FDA finalised its psychedelic trial guidance in July 2026 — active placebos, blinding questionnaires, expectancy controls — and the field is proceeding. **Verify current status before drafting.**
