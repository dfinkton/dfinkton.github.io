## What it's for
The most direct lever on arousal available, and the only practice in this series that works at a 9 out of 10 when nothing cognitive is reachable.

## Practices
Extended exhale. Slow paced breathing. Recognizing when arousal is too high for anything verbal and going here first.

## The physiology, which is unusually well established
A systematic review and meta-analysis of voluntary slow breathing found increased parasympathetic control of the heart — higher RMSSD — at all three timepoints measured: during the practice, immediately after a single session, and after a multi-session intervention. Resonance-frequency breathing at roughly six breaths per minute produces maximal increases in heart rate rhythms; slow breathing between about 4.5 and 6.5 breaths per minute appears to optimally balance the sympatho-vagal response in most adults. Comparable baroreflex stimulation is not achieved during spontaneous breathing or at faster paced rates of ten, twelve, fifteen or sixteen breaths per minute.

## The outcome evidence
Fincham et al. (2023), *Scientific Reports* — meta-analysis of 12 RCTs, 785 adults, self-reported stress: **g = −0.35, 95% CI [−0.55, −0.14], p = 0.0009.** Small-to-medium. Most included studies were judged at moderate risk of bias.

## An evidence-based design framework, and the most practically useful finding here
Effective breath practices **avoided fast-only paces and sessions under five minutes**, and included human-guided training, multiple sessions, and long-term practice. Population, other breath paces, session duration at or above five minutes, and group-versus-individual-versus-at-home delivery were *not* associated with effectiveness. Factors that rendered otherwise promising interventions ineffective: extensive standing, interruptions, involuntary diaphragmatic obstruction, and inadequate training for highly technical practices.

## The honest complication
A single-session RCT with 46 musicians facing a high-stress performance randomised them to slow breathing with biofeedback, slow breathing without, or no treatment. The slow-breathing groups showed significantly greater improvement in HRV measures during performance anticipation (η² = 0.122 and 0.116) — and **adding biofeedback made no difference**, so the breathing did the work. But the intervention groups showed **no overall reduction in self-reported anxiety.** Only participants with high baseline anxiety showed greater reductions than controls (r = 0.379).

So: slow breathing reliably moves the physiology. Whether it moves the felt experience depends on how activated you were to begin with — the same concentration-in-those-who-need-it pattern that appears across this series. Say that plainly rather than promising calm.

## Studies
- Systematic review and meta-analysis of voluntary slow breathing and HRV, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (2022).
- Fincham, G. W., et al. (2023). Effect of breathwork on stress and mental health: A meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials. *Scientific Reports.*
- Single-session HRV biofeedback RCT in musicians (performance anxiety, STAI-S and frequency-domain HRV).
- Goessl et al. (2017) on HRV biofeedback for stress and anxiety — **verify.**
- Slow-paced breathing with HRV biofeedback RCT in panic disorder (n = 55, four weeks, sham-biofeedback active control) — for the vagal/anti-inflammatory pathway. Treat the inflammation mechanism as speculative for a general reader.

## Handling note
Techniques involving cold or physical shock should be described at the level of what the evidence says, not as instructional detail, given who may be reading.
