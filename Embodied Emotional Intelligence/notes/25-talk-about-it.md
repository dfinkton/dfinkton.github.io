## What it's for
Disclosure as a practice — and a correction to the folk theory of it.

## The thesis
If talking helps, it is not ventilation and it may not require a listener. What is left is articulation: forcing a diffuse internal state into language with structure. This makes the essay the long-form version of the naming practice in essay 2. Ten seconds gets one word; twenty minutes gets a sequence and a causal account. Same mechanism, different resolution.

## Practices
Saying it out loud to someone. Writing it down. Telling it as a sequence rather than repeating the grievance. Noticing the difference between organizing an experience and rehearsing it.

## Studies — that people do it
- Rimé, B. (2009). Emotion elicits the social sharing of emotion: Theory and empirical review. *Emotion Review, 1*, 60–85.
- Rimé, B., Finkenauer, C., Luminet, O., Zech, E., & Philippot, P. (1998). Social sharing of emotion: New evidence and new questions. *European Review of Social Psychology, 9*, 145–189.
- Rimé's film experiments (*Cognition and Emotion*, 2000): participants who viewed intensely emotional excerpts engaged in significantly more sharing than those viewing neutral or moderate ones; replicated in everyday life.
- 1975 bereavement study: 88% of bereaved people reported needing to talk about it.

## Studies — that it does not discharge the emotion
- Rimé's own summary: findings were generally not consistent with the view that sharing alleviates the memory of the shared experience.
- Zech, E., & Rimé, B. (2005). Is talking about an emotional experience helpful? Effects on emotional recovery and perceived benefits. *Clinical Psychology and Psychotherapy, 12*, 270–287. — The split between perceived benefit and measured recovery is the finding.
- Finkenauer, C., & Rimé, B. (1998). Socially shared emotional experiences vs. emotional experiences kept secret. *Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 17*, 295–318.

## Studies — the written version, with a shrinking effect
- Pennebaker, J. W., & Beall, S. K. (1986). The original paradigm — four days, fifteen minutes, roughly halved health-center visits over six months.
- Smyth, J. M. (1998). 14 studies, d = 0.47.
- Frattaroli, J. (2006). Experimental disclosure and its moderators: A meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin.* — 146 studies, **d = 0.151.** Stronger effects in high-stress samples and in participants low in optimism.
- Frisina et al. (2004) — small effect on physical health, none on psychological outcomes.
- Meads & Nouwen (2005); Mogk et al. (2006); Reinhold et al. (2018) — no significant effects.
- Pavlacic et al. (2019) — effect specifically for posttraumatic stress.
- "Chasing elusive expressive writing effects: emotion-acceptance instructions and writer engagement improve outcomes" — **disclosure plus acceptance beats disclosure alone.** The series thesis, fifth appearance.

## Studies — where it fails or harms
- Cochrane review of single-session psychological debriefing for preventing PTSD — no evidence of benefit. **State it precisely:** the finding applies to single-session *individual* debriefing, not to all forms of talking after trauma. A published comment on the review makes exactly this point, and the headline is routinely misread.
- van Emmerik, A. A., Kamphuis, J. H., Hulsbosch, A. M., & Emmelkamp, P. M. (2002). Single session debriefing after psychological trauma: A meta-analysis. *The Lancet, 360*, 766–771.

## Co-rumination — verified, and it resolves the essay rather than only complicating it
- Rose, A. J. (2002). Co-rumination in the friendships of girls and boys. *Child Development, 73*, 1830–1843.
- Rose, A. J., Carlson, W., & Waller, E. M. (2007). Prospective associations of co-rumination with friendship and emotional adjustment. *Developmental Psychology, 43.* Six-month longitudinal, middle childhood to mid-adolescence. **For girls, a reciprocal loop: co-rumination predicted increased depressive *and* anxiety symptoms *and* increased positive friendship quality, which then produced more co-rumination. For boys, co-rumination predicted only increased friendship quality — not depression or anxiety.**
- The authors' own implication, which matters in a school: *some girls at risk for internalising problems may go undetected because they have seemingly supportive friendships.*
- Meta-analyses: Spendelow et al. (2017); Dong et al. (2025).
- Contested: one replication in 83 early adolescent girls found co-rumination correlated with depressive symptoms cross-sectionally but did **not** predict longitudinal change, and related *negatively* to social anxiety once depression was controlled. Directionality is disputed.

## The line that makes the essay work
Co-rumination is defined by repeated, extensive problem talk — conjecture about causes, dwelling on associated emotions, **without moving toward problem solving.** Borowski et al. (2024, *Child Development*) state the contrast directly: disclosure is linked only with positive relationship outcomes, whereas co-rumination is linked with positive relationship outcomes *and* negative emotional ones.

So disclosure and co-rumination are separable, and the separator is the same one from essay 6: **concrete articulation versus abstract repetition.** Co-rumination is rumination with a partner. Talking about it helps; going around it again does not — and it feels like closeness while it happens.

## The prayer link
Self-disclosure mediated the mental-health association for colloquial and meditative prayer and did not meditate petitionary or ritual (*Journal of Religion and Health*, 2014, N = 296). People disclosing to something that does not audibly respond still showed the effect — which is the strongest available argument that the listener's response is not the mechanism.
