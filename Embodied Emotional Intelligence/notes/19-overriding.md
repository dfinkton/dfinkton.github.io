## Practices
Delay. Reappraisal. Substitution — same instruction, different vehicle.

## The evidence that suppression is not override — verified

- Butler, E. A., Egloff, B., Wilhelm, F. H., Smith, N. C., Erickson, E. A., & Gross, J. J. (2003). The social consequences of expressive suppression. *Emotion, 3*(1), 48–67. Two studies; unacquainted pairs of women watched an upsetting film and then discussed it, with one member randomly assigned to suppress, respond naturally, or reappraise.
  - **Study 1: suppression alone disrupted communication and raised blood pressure in the suppressor's *partner*.** Reappraisal did not.
  - **Study 2: suppression worsened the regulator's own emotional experience, raised blood pressure in both people, reduced rapport, and inhibited relationship formation.** Partners reported less liking, less rapport, and less desire to be friends.
  - **The finding that should anchor the lesson: suppressors' negative emotions went *up*, not down.** Suppression does not achieve the thing it is for.

- Gross, J. J., & John, O. P. (2003). Individual differences in two emotion regulation processes: implications for affect, relationships, and well-being. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85*(2), 348–362. Five studies. Habitual suppression correlated with lower social connection — less social support, less relationship closeness — **but not with lower liking.** People do not dislike suppressors; they simply do not get close to them. Reappraisal correlated positively with peer-rated connection *and* likeability.

## The mechanism, and why it belongs in this series
Suppression requires continuously monitoring your own expressions, which prevents you from fully attending to the person in front of you and produces behaviour that reads as distracted. It is cognitively taxing in a way reappraisal is not. **Suppression consumes the exact attentional resource that observation runs on** — so it is not merely an inferior strategy, it disables the capacity lessons 1 through 3 are built on.

## A caveat the lesson must carry
Gross and John expected members of lower-status groups to suppress more often, because people with less power monitor and control their expression around those who control resources (Keltner, Gruenfeld & Anderson, 2003). Experimental work on task-oriented dyads finds power moderates these social outcomes. And whether the pattern replicates in non-Western collectivistic settings remains an open empirical question. **Do not write suppression as a character flaw.** For someone in a subordinate position it may be an accurate reading of the room, and the lesson should say so before recommending anything else.

## Reappraisal — verified
- Ochsner, K. N., Bunge, S. A., Gross, J. J., & Gabrieli, J. D. E. (2002). Rethinking feelings: An fMRI study of the cognitive regulation of emotion. *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience.* Reappraising highly negative scenes in unemotional terms reduced subjective negative affect, increased lateral and medial prefrontal activation, and decreased amygdala and medial orbitofrontal activation.
- Buhle, J. T., Silvers, J. A., Wager, T. D., Lopez, R., Onyemekwu, C., Kober, H., Weber, J., & Ochsner, K. N. (2014). Cognitive reappraisal of emotion: A meta-analysis of human neuroimaging studies. *Cerebral Cortex, 24*(11), 2981–2990. Reappraisal recruits frontal and parietal control regions that modulate amygdala responding.
- Reappraisal reduces negative emotion and amygdala activation more effectively than suppression, and at lower cost.

## Two honest limits
- *The mechanism is contested.* Researchers disagree about how reappraisal actually does its work, and at least one study found that reinterpreting a situation did **not** quiet the brain's alarm response at all, despite the technique working. The effect is real; the account of why is not settled.
- *Intensity matters and is understudied.* Work on reappraisal of high- versus low-intensity negative emotion notes that intensity is arguably the most important dimension after valence, and that almost no earlier reappraisal research manipulated it. This is the empirical version of the point lessons 5 and 7 make: a technique that works at a 3 may not be reachable at an 8.

## One finding that supports the embodiment claim
Reappraisal can operate implicitly and automatically rather than deliberately, and unconscious reappraisal is relatively effortless while still reducing emotional reactivity. The prefrontal regions supporting intentional downregulation are also engaged during uninstructed modulation. That is what embodiment looks like in this literature — the same machinery, running without being called.

## Moved from the introduction, 5 Aug 2026
*The whole framing of this lesson.*

### Overriding
Override sounds like suppression. It should not be.

Pushing a feeling down does not remove it. It removes your access to the information while leaving the physical state—the racing heart, the tight chest—running underneath, which is the worst of both. You lose the data and keep the cost.

The version worth practicing is different. You feel the thing fully, you name what it is asking for, and you choose a different action than the one it proposed. There are three main ways to do that—delaying, changing what you understand the situation to mean, and finding a different outlet for what the emotion actually wants. Each gets its own treatment later.

## If-then planning — researched 2026-08-13, relocated from the Lesson 2 register

Four papers read in full. **This is the strongest causal evidence for changing what you do with a
feeling, and it belongs here rather than in the awareness lesson** — awareness is the trigger, this
is the execution. Full source entries in `source-ledger.md`.

### The headline number is much smaller than everyone quotes
**Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024)**, *European Review of Social Psychology* 36(1):2334563,
**642 tests**. Raw **d = .36**; Egger's b = 1.06 ("substantial" bias); **bias-corrected (RoBMA)
d = .15, 95% CI [.08, .22]** — with BF₁₀ = 319 for the effect existing at all.

**The d = .65 in every textbook comes from Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006)**, whose only
publication-bias check was published (.65) vs unpublished (.67), p=.22 — **no funnel plot, no
Egger's test.** That comparison cannot detect a small-study effect because it compares two sets of
small studies. **Read the k column in that paper: every d above 1.0 rests on 3–5 tests**, and the
famous if-component d=.80 comes from k=6 with four of six entries being single studies.

**But the subset that matters here is much larger: affect regulation d = .66, k = 26, I² = 6.3%** —
the least heterogeneous cell in the paper. The small overall figure is health-behaviour studies.

### Intending to regulate does almost nothing. Specifying does.
**Schweiger Gallo, Keil, McCulloch, Rockstroh & Gollwitzer (2009)**, *JPSP* 96(1):11–31. Three
experiments; one sentence of self-talk was the entire manipulation.

- **Disgust:** control 7.13, goal intention 6.89, **if-then plan 5.43** (p<.01 vs both).
  **Control vs goal intention t<1 — the goal alone did nothing.** Neutral and pleasant pictures
  unaffected: the effect was specific to the stimulus named in the "if."
- **Spider fear:** spider-fearful people with a plan rated spider pictures **18.33**; people with
  **no spider fear at all** rated them **18.13** — no difference. Untreated fearful controls 22.51.
- **The brain data:** P1 amplitude at 60–150 ms went from **+2.51 (control) to −1.07 (plan)**.
  **The divergence appears about 100 ms after the picture** — before deliberate control is possible
  (conscious inhibition shows after ~300 ms). Not eye movements; ocular electrodes checked.
- **Caveats, which are real:** P1 omnibus tests were **marginal** (p=.06 parietal, p=.08 occipital);
  only right-hemisphere follow-ups reached p<.05, at **~11 per cell**; all-female samples;
  everything except the ERP is a self-report slider after a sub-second picture; **no follow-up.**
- **The slow wave (552–752 ms) separated *both* self-regulation groups from control.** So goal
  intentions did change brain activity — just late, and with no benefit on any outcome.
  **Intending to regulate is doing something; it is not doing the thing that works.**

### It is the if–then link, not the advice
The obvious objection is that plan participants were simply told what to do. **Bayer & Gollwitzer
(2007) ran that control:** goal intention **plus information about the very strategy** named in the
plan performed **as poorly as the bare goal intention.** Telling someone "stay calm when you see it"
is not the same as having them form "if I see it, then I will stay calm."

### The design rules that survive
- **Specify the trigger for them; let them supply the response.** Four independent literatures
  converge: Sheeran's **self-specified cues d = .16 vs provided .31–1.17**; Torre's fMRI (provided
  labels deactivate the amygdala where free response doesn't, p=.004); Breitwieser's plan-quality
  result; Bayer & Gollwitzer's information-only control above.
- **One plan, not several.** Breitwieser: more than one plan was **worse** (b = −0.24, p = .022).
- **Rehearse the cue–response *link*, don't just write it down.** Milne & Sheeran (2002c):
  rehearsing the link → **87%** acted on the plan; writing the same plan on a **reminder note →
  40%**; control 20%.
- **Contingent format beats scheduling.** "Wait before responding" **.66**; "tell myself to keep
  calm" **.69**.
- **Failure conditions** (Gollwitzer & Sheeran): plans die when the cue rarely occurs, occurs where
  you can't act, the response isn't instrumental or performable, or either half is vague — "eat
  healthily"/"tomorrow" leaves the person "no better off than having merely formed the goal
  intention." Requirements: **precise, viable, instrumental.**
- **The plan is inert without an active goal.** Effects appear only when the superordinate goal is
  live. Commitment itself is *not* the mechanism (no pre/post rise in commitment or self-efficacy;
  Sheeran & Orbell raised screening attendance 33% from a 4.60/5 ceiling) — but a plan for a goal
  the person doesn't hold does nothing.

### The evidence in young people, and the hole in it
**Breitwieser & Reinelt (2026)**, *British Journal of Psychology*, DOI 10.1111/bjop.70065.
**Registered Report** — the strongest-designed meta in this project. k = 52, 42 studies,
N = 12,957, mean age 10.67 (range 4.5–12.99).

**g = 0.31 [0.21, 0.41]** — but **0.18** with outliers removed (I² collapses 65% → 9%) and
**0.47–0.52** with high-risk-of-bias studies removed. PET-PEESE → **0.15**, matching Sheeran
independently; 3PSM/p-curve/z-curve → **0.35–0.39**. **The point estimate is unstable, not merely
small. Any number printed needs its range.** Zero of 52 effects were rated low risk of bias on the
per-protocol question.

**THE FINDING THAT CONSTRAINS THIS LESSON: internal cues k = 1, external cues k = 45.** Verbatim:
"we hypothesized that implementation intentions based on external cues… would be more effective than
those based on **internal cues** (e.g. 'if I feel stressed'), **which require introspection and are
often less specific or salient.** However, **only one study used an internal cue**, thus preventing
any conclusions." The field's preference for external cues is **theoretical convention**, not a
tested comparison. **"If I feel X, then I will Y" is the least-evidenced form of the
best-evidenced technique.**

Other moderators: **younger children benefit more** (−0.05 g per year, p=.022); clinical/ADHD
stronger; **pre-structured planning sheets beat a bare if-then sentence** (+0.32, k=4);
experimenter assistance during planning did nothing (p=.703); gender null; lab vs field null.
**No decay detected — but 35 of 52 studies measured within 24 hours**, and the long-follow-up ones
used booster sessions. Durability is unestablished.

**Coverage gaps:** only **2 of 42 studies** targeted anything interpersonal. **Emotion regulation is
not a domain in this literature at all.** Ceiling effects are a live explanation for null results in
typically-developing children — if-then plans only pay off when the task actually requires
self-regulation.

### The named gap, which is this curriculum's exact problem
**Breitwieser & Reinelt:** "the distinction between simply *having a plan* and learning to use the
*strategy of planning*… **a significant gap in the literature: How can children be taught to use
implementation intentions as a flexible form of self-regulatory control?**" What it requires:
"**monitoring one's own behaviour, identifying the best strategy for a given goal and context and
translating that insight into a well-formed if-then plan.**"

**Gollwitzer & Sheeran said the same thing twenty years earlier**, via Mischel & Shoda: a person who
"knows what kind of social situations elicit aggressive responses in him" can "tailor his
implementation intentions to those critical situations… specify implementation intentions **exactly
where they are needed** — a promising direction for **future studies.**"

**Two generations of this literature have pointed at the awareness → plan sequence. Neither tested
it.** Only **Schaaf et al. 2025** (*Contemporary Educational Psychology* 83:102422, micro-randomised
trial, app) and **Schunk et al. 2022** (*Nature Human Behaviour* 6:1680–1690, classroom workbooks,
N=572) have attempted it. **Both are top of the retrieval queue.**

## Reappraisal: six results, and the moderator that reconciles them

| Source | Result |
|---|---|
| Nozaki-type ordering experiments | Naming a feeling *before* reappraising made it **worse** (ηp²=.14, .07) |
| Compas 2017, N=80,850 | r = **−.05, ns, k=31**; null longitudinally |
| MCII studies | Reappraisal **alone failed** where the if-then plan worked (ηp²=0.21) |
| Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012, k=306 | Overall **0.36**; **physiologically 0.05, ns** |
| Sheeran 2024 | "Keep calm" **.69** vs reappraisal **.36** |
| **Nils & Rimé 2012** | **Reframing worked and still held at 48 hours** (5.70 vs 7.08, p<.01) |

**The last row breaks the pattern, and it breaks it informatively.** What the listener actually said
was not "try to see it differently." It was: your feelings are *"completely normal and legitimate"* —
and then **specific factual counter-evidence** (the footage changed Thai law, closed those markets,
convicted 80 Nazis at Nuremberg).

**Webb's own subtype breakdown says the same thing independently:** perspective-taking **0.45** >
stimulus-focused **0.36** > response-focused **0.23**. The more content-laden the reappraisal, the
better it works; the contentless "accept the response" form is weakest. And Compas's null sits on
questionnaire items measuring *the disposition to reappraise* — not the same object as being handed
a fact.

**Reconciliation: reappraisal fails as a generic instruction to think differently, and works when it
supplies information the person did not have.** Same shape as the provided-beats-self-generated rule
above, reached from a fourth direction. **"Reframe it" is not a teachable move on this evidence.
"Here is what is actually true about this situation" may be — untested in that form**, and the one
study that did it used an atrocity film rather than a personal provocation.

## Attending to a feeling is three operations with three different signs
From **Webb, Miles & Sheeran (2012)**, k=306 (Egger's p=.44, fail-safe N=9,285, no publication bias):

**A published exchange, resolved 14 Aug 2026 — and the resolution is "normal methods disagreement,
findings stand."** Augustine & Hemenover (2013), *Psych Bulletin* 139(3):725–729, argued the inclusion
criteria and effect-size selection were inconsistent and that magnitudes depend on the chosen
comparator. **Webb, Miles & Sheeran replied** (139(3):730–734, PMID 23607434): the concerns "appear to
have accrued from **misunderstandings of our inclusion criteria** or from **disagreements with
methodological decisions that are crucial to the validity of meta-analysis**," their findings are
"consistent with theoretical predictions and previous reviews," and — **the part that matters for this
curriculum** — they "present new evidence that the effect sizes… are generalizable," demonstrating
that their estimates "**reveal how well these strategies predict important emotional outcomes over
1 year.**"

**So: use the directions and the rank ordering below with confidence. Do not adjudicate the
magnitudes — that argument is between two research teams and has no bearing on what this curriculum
teaches.** Worth noting the prior meta with a different method exists: Augustine & Hemenover (2009).
- **Using the feeling as a cue to act: +0.44 to +0.86**
- **Reliving the feeling: −0.14**
- **Analysing why you have it: −0.34** (see lesson 6 — this is rumination)

These were conflated in the Lesson 2 register for several days. They are not one thing.
Also: **distraction +0.27, and active distraction beats passive (0.40 vs 0.22).** Expressive
suppression: behaviour **+0.97**, experience **+0.10**, physiology **−0.22** — it changes what people
see, not much of what you feel, and costs you physiologically.
