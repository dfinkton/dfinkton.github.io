## What it's for
A series called *embodied* with no lesson on moving the body is a structural oddity. Exercise has moderate, consistent effects on mood and executive function.

## Practices
Regular movement as a regulation decision rather than a fitness one. Movement during or after high arousal.

## The acute effect — one bout, same day
A meta-analysis of people with depression found a favourable affective response following a single bout of physical activity, **SMD = 0.585, 95% CI [0.456, 0.714]**, consistent across affective state types, exercise conditions and participant characteristics, and robust to protocol changes and publication bias. **Important caveat the authors state themselves: only within-person pre-post changes were examined, with no comparison to control conditions.**

A separate meta-analysis of acute exercise across populations found general mood improved from pre- to post-exercise (g = 0.336), anxiety improved (g = 0.497), and depressive symptoms improved (g = 0.407) — with **substantial, statistically significant heterogeneity in every analysis**, unexplained by health status, exercise intensity, mode, usual activity level, or weight status. Nobody knows who responds and who doesn't.

## The chronic effect
Schuch et al. (2016), 25 studies and 1,487 participants, found a significant antidepressant effect, strongest in major depression. Heissel et al. (2023), 41 studies and 2,264 patients with MDD, confirmed a positive effect on depression levels. Physical activity is recognised as a primary or adjunct treatment for depression by the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists, the European Psychiatric Association, and the British Association for Psychopharmacology.

## Dose-response, stated as a finding rather than a prescription
Better outcomes were associated with three or more sessions per week, durations over thirty minutes, across periods under twelve weeks, combining forms of aerobic exercise.

## Where the popular claim overreaches
The executive-function story is smaller and narrower than usually presented. In adults with depression: working memory improved (ES = 0.33, 95% CI [0.04, 0.61]), inhibition did not reach significance (ES = 0.28), and cognitive flexibility was trivial (ES = 0.09). A Bayesian meta-analysis of acute exercise in healthy young adults — 651 effect sizes, 113 studies, 4,390 participants — found **g = 0.13**. Small. So: exercise moves working memory a little, not general thinking a lot.

## And one null worth stating
Exercise did not show a statistically significant effect on suicidal tendencies or suicide risk. **If this appears in the lesson, handle it carefully — it is a limit on what exercise does, not a comment on anyone's situation, and the lesson should point toward support rather than leaving the reader with a bare negative.**
