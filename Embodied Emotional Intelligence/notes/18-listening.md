### Listening
With observation in place, you reach the actual choice: follow the instruction, or don't.

The default should be to listen to it, and this is where most writing about emotional skill leads to emotional suppression—reducing the information you get from your emotions. It treats emotions as problems to be managed rather than information to be used.

An emotion is almost always right that something is happening. Anger reliably indicates that something crossed a line you hold. Fear reliably indicates that some part of you detected risk. Sadness reliably indicates loss. Dismissing the signal because it is inconvenient means discarding accurate information about your own life, and people who do this again and again end up unable to tell what they want or when they are being mistreated.

What is far less reliable is the specific action the emotion proposes. Anger correctly identifies a violation and then the programmed habit recommends escalation, which frequently makes the violation worse. Fear correctly identifies risk and then the learned response recommends avoidance, which often costs more than the risk would have.

So the emotion is a witness, not a judge. Take its testimony seriously. Treat the punishment it recommends as one option among several.

Listen more than you probably do to care, curiosity and joy. These push toward things that are usually worth doing, and most people override them far more often than they need to.
